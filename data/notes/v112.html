Below are extensive satsang notes for Video index 127, based on the transcript you provided. This video explains verse 112, where the Guru describes the fruit of samkit: the arising of right conduct and the attainment of complete dispassion.

Video 127 Notes: Verse 112 — From Intensified Samkit to Complete Dispassion

1) Link from the previous verses

The discourse begins by placing verse 112 in the sequence of verses 108–113.

The Guru has already shown the seeker’s journey step by step:

VersStage Spiritual Meaning e Explained

108 Preparation Four virtues of a true aspirant

109 First samkit Vyavhar samkit: right faith based on Sadguru’s teachings

110 Second samkit Shuddh samkit: experiential faith, glimpse of the Self

111 Third samkit Parmarth samkit: highest right faith, constant inward flow

112 Right conduct Gradual destruction of conduct-deluding passions

113 Omniscience Kevaljnan

The speaker explains that verses 109–111 were emotionally rich because they dealt with the seeker’s relationship with the Sadguru, the first glimpse of the Self, and the inner state of an Enlightened One.

Verse 112 becomes more technical because now the Guru moves from right faith to right conduct.

The focus shifts from:

What is samkit? to What does samkit produce?

The answer:

The fruit of samkit is vitragta — complete dispassion.

2) Connection to the 14 gunasthanaks

The speaker connects the six verses with the 14 stages of spiritual development.

Verse 108

The seeker is still in the 1st gunasthanak, but on the borderline. He is not yet Self-realised, but he has become intensely worthy.

Verse 109

The seeker is still in the 1st gunasthanak, but on the very frontier. He has vyavhar samkit, right faith based on the Sadguru’s teachings.

Verse 110

The seeker reaches the 4th gunasthanak, because he has attained shuddh samkit, experiential faith.

Verse 111

This can be connected with the 4th or 5th gunasthanak, but since the focus is still samkit, the speaker says we can keep it mainly as the 4th.

Verse 112

Now the verse covers a rapid journey from the 5th to the 12th gunasthanak.

Verse 113

The next verse reaches the 13th gunasthanak, omniscience.

The speaker highlights the compression:

We have covered only up to the 4th or 5th gunasthanak by verse 111, but only two verses remain: 112 and 113. So verse 112 must rapidly cover many stages.

Verse 112 therefore deals with the gradual purification of conduct from the 5th to the 12th gunasthanak.

3) Verse 112: word-by-word meaning

The verse being explained is:

Vardhaman samkit thai, taley mithyabhas;
Uday thay charitrano, vitragpad vas.

“Vardhaman”

Means: increasing, growing, intensifying.

Here it refers to right faith becoming stronger and more powerful.

The seeker’s samkit is no longer weak or occasional. It is deepening through abidance in the Self.

“Samkit thai”

Means: as right faith increases or intensifies.

This refers to the growth of samyak darshan, especially after the experience of the Self.

“Taley”

Means: removes, eliminates, destroys, causes to subside.

“Mithyabhas”

In this verse, the speaker explains it as conduct delusion.

Faith delusion has already been removed through samkit. Now what remains is conduct delusion — the passions.

So “mithyabhas” here refers to:

  • attachment
  • aversion
  • conduct-deluding passions
  • feelings under the influence of charitra mohaniya karma

“Uday thay”

Means: arises, appears, manifests, dawns.

“Charitrano”

Means: of right conduct.

So “uday thay charitrano” means:

Right conduct arises.

“Vitragpad vas”

Means: the soul attains the state of complete dispassion.

Vitragta is the complete state of freedom from attachment and aversion.

4) Short meaning of the verse

As the state of right faith intensifies, the feelings arising from conduct-deluding karma are gradually removed. With the dawn of right conduct, the soul abides in the state of complete dispassion.

5) What is the fruit of samkit?

The speaker makes the main point very clearly:

The fruit of samkit is vitragta.

Right faith does not remain merely a belief. It begins to transform conduct.

When samkit becomes stronger, passions begin to weaken. As passions weaken, right conduct arises. As right conduct becomes complete, the soul reaches vitragta, complete dispassion.

So the flow is:

Samkit intensifies → passions decrease → right conduct arises → complete dispassion is attained

6) Purity in faith is instant; purity in conduct is gradual

This is one of the key teachings of the discourse.

The speaker says:

Purity in faith occurs instantly. Purity in conduct occurs gradually.

Purity in faith

When shuddh samkit arises, right belief becomes pure immediately. The faith-deluding karma is destroyed or overcome.

The seeker now knows experientially:

I am the pure Soul.

Purity in conduct

But conduct does not become perfect immediately.

Passions have been weakened, but not completely destroyed. The seeker must gradually overcome subtle and subtler forms of passions.

So:

  • right faith becomes pure instantly
  • right conduct becomes pure gradually
  • complete right conduct is attained at the 12th gunasthanak
  • omniscience comes afterward in the 13th

This distinction is central to understanding verse 112.

7) Faith delusion is gone; conduct delusion remains

In previous verses, the Guru had explained two forms of mohaniya karma:

1. Darshan mohaniya — faith-deluding karma

2. Charitra mohaniya — conduct-deluding karma

With samkit, darshan mohaniya is removed.

But charitra mohaniya still remains. This means:

  • the seeker has right belief
  • the seeker knows the truth
  • but subtle habits, passions, and tendencies are still active

Thus verse 112 focuses on the gradual removal of charitra mohaniya, conduct delusion.

8) The four categories of passions

The speaker explains that conduct-deluding passions are described in Jain scriptures in four categories.

The four passions are:

1. Krodh — anger

2. Maan — pride / arrogance

3. Maya — deceit

4. Lobh — greed

But each of these passions can appear in different intensities. The scriptures classify them into four levels.

1. Anantanubandhi kashay — the grossest passions

This is the most intense and most binding category.

The speaker explains that when these passions arise, they create a loop. One cycle of bondage creates another, and the soul remains trapped for countless lives.

Characteristics:

  • very gross
  • extremely intense
  • creates long cycles of bondage
  • prevents samyak darshan itself
  • keeps the soul in the loop of passions

This category is destroyed at the time of samyak darshan.

So by the time verse 112 begins, anantanubandhi passions are already gone.

2. Apratyakhyani kashay — passions that prevent partial vows

These passions are less gross than anantanubandhi but still powerful.

They do not prevent right faith, but they prevent the seeker from taking even small vows.

Examples given:

  • “I cannot stop eating at night.”
  • “I cannot even do Navkarsi.”
  • “What if I feel hungry?”
  • “I love the Atmarpit way of life, but what if I cannot stay steady?”
  • “I want spirituality, but I cannot commit to even a partial vow.”

This category allows the person to know what is right, but does not allow him to do it.

3. Pratyakhyani kashay — passions that prevent complete vows

This category is subtler.

The seeker can take small vows, but cannot take complete or lifelong vows.

Examples:

  • “I avoid root vegetables, but I don’t want to take a vow.”
  • “I practise celibacy, but I don’t want a lifelong vow.”
  • “I can do some restraint, but I cannot embrace complete renunciation.”
  • “I can take partial vows, but not complete vows.”

These passions prevent full renunciation or complete vows.

4. Sanjvalan kashay — very subtle passions

These are the subtlest passions.

They are so subtle that ordinary intellect cannot even grasp them.

Characteristics:

  • almost invisible
  • very faint disturbance
  • subtle likes-dislikes
  • very short-lived
  • prevents complete dispassion
  • remains until the higher stages

These are finally destroyed on the path to the 12th gunasthanak.

9) The sequence of destruction

The speaker explains the process:

At samyak darshan

The grossest passions, anantanubandhi, are destroyed.

This is why samyak darshan becomes possible.

As samkit intensifies

The seeker destroys:

  • apratyakhyani passions
  • pratyakhyani passions

Now he can embrace complete vows or the state of monkhood.

After right conduct arises

The seeker works on the final category:

  • sanjvalan passions

When even these subtle passions are destroyed, the seeker reaches the 12th gunasthanak, called kshinmoha — the stage where delusion is destroyed.

10) “I do not want anything from the world”

The speaker gives the inner resolve of the one who has attained samyak darshan:

I do not want anything from the world. I just want to abide in myself.

This is the foundation for right conduct.

When the seeker truly understands that nothing in the world can give real happiness, worldly expectation drops. He no longer demands change from the world. Instead, he accepts situations and turns within.

The speaker says:

Why expect a change? Rather accept a change, because I do not want anything from the world. I just want to abide in my true nature.

This is the inner movement from right faith toward right conduct.

11) Right faith getting intensified

“Vardhaman samkit” means that samkit becomes stronger.

It intensifies through:

  • more abidance in the Self
  • less interest in worldly activities
  • reduced attachment and aversion
  • stronger inner resolve
  • increasing purity
  • deeper awareness

The more right faith grows, the more conduct-delusion loses its strength.

12) The seeker loses interest in worldly activities

From the time right faith arises, the seeker begins to lose interest in worldly activities, at least in their grossest forms.

This does not mean he becomes inactive. It means he loses fascination for worldly pleasures, ego, indulgence, and passions.

He becomes more interested in:

  • peace
  • abidance
  • dispassion
  • inner stillness
  • purity
  • spiritual effort

When passions erupt, he no longer wants to justify them. He wants to return to his peace centre.

13) Bondage of karma starts reducing

With samyak darshan, the bondage of karma begins to reduce.

The speaker explains:

  • the impact of conduct-deluding karma decreases
  • passions are not destroyed immediately
  • but their intensity starts reducing
  • karmic bondage steadily goes down

This is the beginning of true spiritual progress.

14) The Ayambil examples: how passions obstruct vows

The speaker uses humorous and practical examples to explain the categories of passions.

Shiv and Ayambil

If the Guru says, “Let’s do Ayambil tomorrow,” different inner reactions reveal different levels of passion.

If the person rejects the idea completely:

“What rubbish! Why Ayambil now? It isn’t even Oli.”

This reflects the grossest passions — the mind cannot even accept the value of restraint.

If the person agrees but wants exceptions:

“Can I at least have tea or coffee?”

This reflects passions that still obstruct deeper restraint.

If the person agrees but hesitates about full discipline:

This reflects passions preventing complete vows.

The point is not only about Ayambil. It is about how the inner passions respond when restraint is asked of us.

15) The three-item food example

The speaker says they had been eating only three items since April 15 and initially felt:

See, we can survive on three items.

But then he realised that the three items were being made so tasty that it did not feel like austerity.

So merely reducing the number of items is not the whole point.

The real austerity is:

  • reducing taste attachment
  • eating less than capacity
  • reducing dependence on deliciousness
  • not letting the mind indulge in food

He jokes that they may need to add a powder or remove one spice daily to make food less tasty.

The deeper point:

External austerity is not enough if attachment to taste remains intact.

16) The female ascetic with 1400 Ayambils

The speaker gives a powerful example of a female ascetic he met.

She was on her 1400th Ayambil.

Details:

  • She had been doing Ayambil continuously for years.
  • She ate only two items: rice and water.
  • She ate only at 4 pm.
  • She did ekalthanu — sitting in one place, moving only hands and jaws.
  • Once she found the rice tasty.
  • From then on, she added powder so that even the taste of rice would not attract her.

This example shows extreme vigilance toward taste attachment.

She said:

When extremely hungry, even rice seems tasty.

The teaching:

Attachment can arise even in austerity. Therefore the seeker must remain alert.

17) The 72-hour attachment principle

The speaker gives a psychological principle:

If you stay with any object or person for more than 72 hours, attachment begins.

Examples:

  • a jail prisoner may cry while leaving jail after years
  • a monk should not stay in one place for more than three days
  • after three days in a room, one starts feeling:
    • my bed
    • my table
    • my spoon
    • my room
    • my house

The point:

Association breeds attachment unless awareness is strong.

This is why spiritual disciplines like movement, restraint, and non-possession are prescribed.

18) “Uday” of right conduct

The verse says:

Uday thay charitrano Right conduct arises.

The speaker explains the word uday beautifully.

Usually, uday is used in two contexts:

1. Karmoday

The manifestation or fruition of karma.

Example:

  • “Bad karma has manifested.”
  • “Good karma has manifested.”

2. Suryoday

Sunrise.

But sunrise does not mean the sun is newly created. The sun existed; it was simply out of sight. When it appears in the sky, we call it sunrise.

Similarly, right conduct was always part of the soul’s true nature, but it was hidden by delusion and passions. When delusion is reduced, right conduct appears.

So “uday thay charitrano” means:

Right conduct manifests in the heart.

19) What is right conduct?

Right conduct is not merely external conduct.

It is not only:

  • changing clothes
  • changing name
  • changing lifestyle
  • becoming formally initiated
  • taking outer vows

These may help, but true right conduct is:

Abidance in the Self.

The speaker says:

Externally, one may embrace monkhood. Internally, the state of true monkhood arises.

True monkhood is when the seeker revels in pure awareness and becomes steady in the Self.

20) Prabhushriji’s statement about Param Krupalu Dev

The speaker quotes Prabhushriji:

“I see You neither as a householder nor a renunciate. I see You as One abiding in the Soul.”

This is true monkhood.

The teaching:

Monkhood is not merely a change of dress or social identity. True monkhood is abidance in the Self.

A householder can have the inner state of a monk if he abides in the Self. A monk may wear the attire but still not have true monkhood if inner abidance is absent.

21) The 48-minute reference

The speaker explains that a true monk or advanced seeker withdraws into the Self repeatedly, even within a short period.

He says:

Within 48 minutes — or keep it as one hour if 48 minutes is difficult — such a soul withdraws many times and touches deep states.

Sometimes this withdrawal may happen only for half a second. Outsiders may not notice it. But a worthy close disciple may see it in the eyes.

He says disciples of Param Krupalu Dev experienced this:

Even while speaking, He would suddenly withdraw inward. Perhaps only for half a second, but He would touch deep meditative states.

This shows the living reality of right conduct.

22) From sixth gunasthanak to the twelfth

When apratyakhyani and pratyakhyani passions are destroyed, the seeker can embrace complete vows and monkhood.

This corresponds to the 6th gunasthanak.

But subtle passions remain. These are sanjvalan passions.

The seeker must overcome these final subtle passions to reach complete dispassion.

23) The last category: Sanjvalan passions

Sanjvalan passions are very subtle.

The speaker says:

  • they may not be visible
  • they may not even be grasped by the intellect
  • they create very subtle disturbance
  • they prevent complete dispassion

The monk has virtually overcome all passions, but technically not yet completely.

He is on the 6th gunasthanak but must reach the 12th.

24) The two ladders: Upsham shreni and Kshapak shreni

The speaker introduces the idea of two spiritual ladders:

1. Upsham shreni

The ladder of pacification.

Here passions are pacified, but not destroyed. Because they are only suppressed or pacified, the soul can fall again.

2. Kshapak shreni

The ladder of destruction.

Here passions are destroyed.

The speaker says he does not see the upsham shreni emphasis in Apurva Avsar; there the focus is on kshapak shreni — the destructive ladder.

This is the ladder by which the seeker rapidly destroys the final subtle passions.

25) The cucumber and tomato cutting example

To explain the speed of the kshapak shreni, the speaker jokes:

The final passions must be cut swiftly — like women slicing cucumbers, not like men slicing tomatoes.

Men slice tomatoes so slowly and carefully that even mangoes are not sliced with such care.

The point:

The final destruction of passions on kshapak shreni is rapid, swift, and decisive.

26) Kshinmoha gunasthanak: the 12th stage

When all conduct-deluding karmas are destroyed, the soul reaches the 12th gunasthanak.

This is called:

Kshinmoha gunasthanak the stage where delusion is destroyed

At this stage:

  • all conduct-deluding passions are destroyed
  • complete dispassion is attained
  • the soul becomes vitrag
  • right conduct is complete

But omniscience has not yet arisen. That comes in verse 113.

27) Vitragta is not yet omniscience

The speaker makes an important distinction:

At the end of verse 112, the soul attains:

Vitragta — total dispassion

But not yet:

Kevaljnan — omniscience

Omniscience comes in the next verse.

So verse 112 completes conduct purification, not knowledge perfection.

The sequence is:

Complete destruction of delusion → complete dispassion → omniscience soon after

28) Summary of the full journey in verse 112

The entire verse can be understood as follows:

1. The seeker has already attained samkit.

2. The grossest passions have already been destroyed.

3. As samkit intensifies, the remaining conduct-deluding passions weaken.

4. Passions that prevent partial vows are destroyed.

5. Passions that prevent complete vows are destroyed.

6. Right conduct arises.

7. The seeker embraces true monkhood, externally or internally.

8. Subtle passions remain.

9. The seeker climbs the kshapak shreni.

10. All subtle passions are destroyed.

11. The soul reaches the 12th gunasthanak.

12. Complete dispassion is attained.

29) The whole verse in one flow

Vardhaman samkit thai As right faith grows and intensifies,

Taley mithyabhas conduct delusion and passions are removed,

Uday thay charitrano right conduct arises,

Vitragpad vas and the soul abides in the state of complete dispassion.

30) Main distinction: belief and conduct

Aspect Purification Faith / belief Instant

Conduct Gradual

Faith delusion Removed through samkit

Conduct Removed through increasing samkit and right delusion conduct

Final fruit Vitragta

This is one of the clearest ways to remember the discourse.

31) Practical application for seekers

1. Do not think samkit ends the journey

Right faith is not the final goal. It must mature into conduct.

2. Check if passions are reducing

The real fruit of right faith is reduction of passions. If passions are not reducing, samkit has not become powerful in life.

3. External vows are not enough

The important question is not just, “Have I taken a vow?” The question is, “Have I reduced attachment, aversion, and taste for the world?”

4. Austerity must reduce attachment

Doing Ayambil, limiting food, fasting, silence, or discipline must reduce passions. If taste, ego, or pride remain, the inner work is incomplete.

5. True monkhood is abidance in the Self

The real rise of right conduct is not merely outward renunciation. It is the rise of Self-abidance.

6. Subtle passions matter

Even after gross passions are gone, subtle tendencies remain. The final journey is about subtle purification.

7. Complete dispassion precedes omniscience

Before kevaljnan, the soul must reach complete dispassion.

34) Verse 112 in relation to verse 113

Verse 112 takes the seeker to:

Vitragta — complete dispassion 12th gunasthanak — kshinmoha

Verse 113 will take the seeker to:

Kevaljnan — omniscience 13th gunasthanak

So verse 112 completes the destruction of mohaniya karma, while verse 113 reveals the manifestation of omniscience.

35) One-line summary
