0) Context in the series (why this verse comes now)

  • We are in the Third Fundamental Truth discussion: the soul is the doer of karmas (karmic bondage).
  • The disciple, influenced by other philosophies, tried to prove: “Soul is non-doer”.
  • In verse 72 (latter half), the disciple’s argument is:

God inspires the soul to do/acquire karmas, so the soul is not responsible and is unbound. Therefore, all responsibility rests on God (God as creator + controller + prompter).

This video covers the Guru’s answer to that exact claim.

1) Disciple’s claim being addressed (0:00–1:27)

What the disciple is asserting

  • God created the world / universe.
  • Everything happening occurs according to God’s will.
  • All karmas done by worldly beings are through God’s compelling inspiration.
  • Therefore:
    • soul has no fault in doing karmas,
    • soul is not the doer,
    • soul is not the creator of karmic bondage,
    • soul is unbound.

What’s implied (even if not explicitly stated)

  • If God prompts, then “my karma” is not really mine.
  • Liberation-path effort becomes questionable because the responsibility shifts away from the soul.

2) Guru’s direct reply — the verse (1:59 onward)

The Guru’s response is delivered as a tight, two-part refutation.

Core verse line-by-line (as explained in transcript)

Line 1

“Karta Ishwar koi nahi”
  • There is no God who is a doer, in the sense of:

1. creator of the universe

2. running everything by “His will”

3. prompter/inspirer of worldly beings’ actions

Line 2

“Ishwar shuddha swabhav”
  • “God” (Ishwar) = the one who has attained the purest state.

Then the verse moves to a conditional:

Line 3–4 (the “if you insist” part)

If you insist God is the inspirer (prerak) of actions/karmas: “Ishwar doshprabhav”

  • then God becomes deemed flawed / full of defects / contradictory,
  • and loses Godliness (even if you keep the label “God”).

3) The Guru’s definition of “God” (2:41–4:00) The key distinction: nature vs state

The speaker explains a crucial Jain framing:

  • If you speak from nature (swabhav):
    • the soul’s inherent nature is pure → in that sense, the soul is “divine”.
  • If you speak from state (avastha):
    • “God” refers to the soul that has achieved the purest possible state:
      • siddha (accomplished)
      • “nothing more to do”
      • “nothing more to achieve”
      • liberation completed.

So “God” is not a cosmic manager; “God” is a fully purified soul.

4) Why “God as doer/inspirer” destroys the very meaning of God (4:00–8:40)

The inner logic: doing → desire → imperfection → impurity

The transcript lays out a chain:

1. Doing implies desire

2. Desire implies imperfection

3. Imperfection implies loss of purity

4. Therefore, a flawless God would not engage in worldly “doing”.

So:

  • If you make God a doer/inspirer, you push God into:
    • desire
    • preferences
    • partiality
    • contradictions
    • responsibility entanglements

Result: God “remains” as a word, but Godliness collapses.

5) Connection to Anandghanji Maharaj (4:33–7:20)

The speaker notes Param Krupalu Dev uses wording aligned with Anandghanji Maharaj’s idea:

  • “Some say the world is God’s leela (play).”
  • But:
    • “Doshrahitne leela navi ghate” A faultless God doesn’t “fit” into worldly play.

He explains Param Krupalu Dev’s phrasing:

  • Anandghanji uses doshvilas (full of flaws)
  • Atmasiddhi uses doshprabhav (deemed flawed)
  • The rhyme is also noted: swabhav / prabhav.

Point: Calling the world “God’s play” makes God “flawed-by-association.”

6) What is God supposed to be? (10:58–13:40)

The speaker emphasizes a “minimum, generic” concept of God across religions:

God should be:

  • purna shuddha (completely pure)
  • immaculate, spotless, pristine
  • divinely majestic (not about muscles/appearance—about inner divinity)
  • “wholly holy”
  • supreme (sarvoch)
  • immeasurable greatness

Then he says: it is patently absurd to imagine such a being:

  • interfering
  • intervening
  • inspiring/inducing actions

Quiz hook here

He explicitly says the “I’s” will come:

  • Interfere
  • Intervene
  • Inspire
  • (and he adds: Induce)

7) The “prompter God” becomes ridiculous and contradictory (13:40–18:56)

He uses everyday examples to show how absurd the prompter model becomes:

If God is inspiring everyone:

  • God must “script” different instructions for different people,
  • even contradictory instructions:
    • “You stay calm”
    • “You slap back”
    • “You respect”
    • “You retaliate”

This turns God into a cosmic micromanager with infinite duties.

Then comes the contradiction:

  • God inspires someone to do wrong…
  • then inspires them to repent…
  • then why inspire the wrong in the first place?

Conclusion: This concept makes God look:

  • inconsistent
  • foolish
  • and ethically compromised.

He calls the verse’s words hard-hitting:

  • “Ishwar doshprabhav” = you end up flawing the flawless and staining the spotless.

8) Important clarification: God can be support, but not the cause of your karmas (18:38–19:40)

He makes a clean boundary:

  • You may take:
    • God’s support (avlamban / aadhar),
    • God’s teachings / ajna,
    • inspiration from the ideal of Godhood,
  • to get rid of karmas.

But:

  • God has nothing to do with creating your karmas.
  • God is not the doer of your actions.

This preserves both:

  • devotion (bhakti)
  • and responsibility (purusharth).

9) Why people love “creator-regulator-judge God” (20:34–29:40)

He shifts to the psychology/social side:

Many religions conceive God as:

1. creator

2. regulator

3. dispenser of justice

Why people like this emotionally:

  • It feels comforting to believe:
    • “If I please God, I’ll get comfort”
    • “God will forgive my sins”
    • “God will protect me”
  • People prefer a doer-God (who smiles, talks, blesses) over a dispassionate knower.

A sharp warning (also applies to Guru-concept)

Even if someone is Jain philosophically, emotionally they may:

  • keep God “clean/dispassionate” as an idea,
  • but project the same “doer expectations” onto Guru:
    • “shower blessings”
    • “fulfill desires”
    • “endorse my nonsense”

He ties this to “God-fearing vs God-loving”:

  • Many are trained to fear God (as a control mechanism)
  • But a mature path is to become God-loving:
    • love God’s majestic state,
    • not “what God can do for me”.

Bhakti teaching

He mentions:

  • premlakshana bhakti (loving devotion)
  • Love for God produces:
    • reduction of likes/dislikes
    • detachment from worldly objects

He gives a strong test:

  • If detachment isn’t growing, don’t call it “love”—call it enjoyment.

10) The Guru’s “four questions” that dismantle creator/prompter God (34:45 onward)

He says he’ll end with four questions—these are the core logical demolition.

Q1) “Why?” (34:53–41:49)

  • If God is fulfilled, blissful, desireless:
    • Why would God create the world?
  • He references the idea: “Ekoham bahusyam” (“I was one, I want to be many”)
    • He argues this implies boredom/curiosity/desire
    • which contradicts a fulfilled God.

He uses humor:

  • “Was God tired of bliss?”
  • “Did God want entertainment?”
  • “Creating drama implies inner agitation.”

He reinforces:

  • Humans created this “doer God” concept often to avoid responsibility:
    • “It was God’s will” becomes an escape from ownership.

Q2) “How?” (42:12–47:00)

If God created the universe, then how exactly?

He pushes the classic causality analysis:

Option A: God is the material cause (upadan)

  • Then God “becomes” the universe.
  • But then:
    • if God is conscious,
    • material atoms would have to carry consciousness too (effect must exist in cause).

Option B: God is only the instrumental cause (like a potter)

  • Then material already exists separately.
  • New questions explode:
    • Where was God before the world existed?
    • What process did God use?
    • How long did creation take?
    • What came first?
    • If later God wants destruction—where do substances go if they can’t be destroyed?

He uses this to show: the model produces endless incoherent questions.

Q3) “Why is God partial?” (47:08–53:00)

If God inspires actions:

  • why does God inspire some to do good, some to do bad?
  • why inspire wrong actions and then repentance?
  • why create suffering, inequality, pandemics, disability?

He uses the Kasab example to show how the “prompter” concept spirals into moral absurdity:

  • Who prompted the violence?
  • Who prompted capture?
  • Who prompted regret?
  • Who decided punishment?
  • What “joy” does God get from misery?

This destroys the idea of a compassionate, flawless deity who “scripts” karma.

Q4) “Who designed God?” (53:00–54:43)

He addresses the “design implies designer” argument:

  • If world needs a designer → you say God designed it.
  • Then: who designed God?
  • That creates infinite regress (designer of designer of designer…)
  • Eventually, people say:
    • “God always existed.”

He replies:

  • If you can accept “always existed” for God,
  • why not accept that the world/substances also always existed?
  • So “creator God” is unnecessary and illogical.

11) Final conclusion of the verse (54:43–55:06)

  • The doctrines “prakriti alone does bondage” and “God does bondage” are both shown illogical.
  • Therefore:
    • the soul is the doer of karmas.
  • This restores:
    • moral/spiritual responsibility,
    • and makes liberation meaningful (because what you do inwardly can be undone).

12) Compact revision sheet (quick study)

Verse 77 (core meaning)

  • God is not a doer/creator/prompter.
  • God = soul in the purest state (siddha).
  • If you insist God prompts actions → you make God flawed (doshprabhav).

Key refutation tools used

1. Doing implies desire → desire implies imperfection → cannot fit a flawless God.

2. Creator model fails under: Why? How? Partiality? Who created God?

3. People emotionally want a protective doer-God; Jain framing keeps God as knower, not doer.

4. If God were a doer, Godliness collapses.

Quiz hooks from the talk

  • “Four questions”: Why / How / Partiality / Who created God
  • “I-words”: interfere / intervene / inspire / induce
  • Key phrase: flaw the flawless, stain the spotless
  • Distinction: nature vs state (God = state of complete purity)
