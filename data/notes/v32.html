• Verses 24–31: Param Krupalu Dev described two types of matārthī (bigots):
    • Kriyājada matārthī – the mechanical ritualist (lots of vows, fasts, rituals; no inner work).
    • Śuṣk-jñānī matārthī – the dry intellectualist (lots of knowledge, lofty words; no inner work).
  • Both:
    • Are unworthy (an-adhikārī) for parmārth – the supreme truth: Self-realisation / liberation.
    • Are very different externally, but inside they share the same inner defects.
  • This verse now:
    • Shows the common inner traits of both bigots.
    • Focuses on inner qualities, because externals differ (one “over-does”, one “negates”), but the inner root is the same.

2. The “total” description of a bigot

Param Krupalu Dev lists four fundamental virtues, but in this verse they are shown in negation:

Nahi (there is no)

1. Kaṣāyni upśāntatā

2. Antar vairāgya

3. Saralpanu

4. Madhyasthata

  • Absence of these 4 makes one a matārthī.
  • Presence of these 4 makes one an ātmārthī (true seeker).
  • This single verse:
    • Gives a complete profile of a bigot / hypocrite.
    • Covers heart, mind, and character:
      • Heart full of desires
      • Mind full of distractions
      • Character full of prejudices

That’s why the speaker says: even if verses 24–31 didn’t exist, this verse alone is a total description of a bigot.

3. Why he is called “durbhāgya” (unfortunate)

  • He has everything external:
    • Right God, right Guru, right scriptures
    • Exposure to proper rituals, vows, austerities
    • Even high philosophy from the absolute viewpoint (soul’s pure nature, etc.).
  • But due to absence of these four inner virtues:
    • He remains a bigot, not a seeker.
    • He may get merit and good heavens, but not Self-realisation/liberation.
  • So Param Krupalu Dev compassionately calls him:
    • “E matārthī durbhāgya” – “This bigot is truly unfortunate.”
    • Like someone sitting by a pond of sweet water and still dying of thirst.

4. Spiritual level vs spiritual activity

When we say someone’s “spiritual level is high”, it does not mean:

  • How many hours of meditation,
  • How much seva or bhakti,
  • How much scripture they read.

Those are activities, not level.

Spiritual level = presence of certain necessary virtues.

  • A person can be:
    • Very sweet, helpful, soft-spoken, etc.
    • Yet not considered spiritually evolved if these 4 key virtues are missing.
  • Conversely, one may lack many worldly skills (like changing a tyre or driving a car) but can still be spiritually elevated if these inner virtues are present.

So this verse is really giving:

The minimum inner qualifications to be even on the path.

5. The four fundamental virtues (in negation here)

1) Kaṣāyni upśāntatā nahi

No subsidence of passions

Kaṣāy = inner passions:

  • Krodh – anger
  • Mān – pride
  • Māyā – deceit
  • Lobh – greed Or more simply: rāg–dveṣ (attachment–aversion).

Upśāntatā = subsidence, calming down, weakening:

  • Passions still exist, but:
    • They should be much weaker,
    • Less violent, less frequent, less sticky.

For a true seeker, what’s expected is “weakened passions”, not zero passions (zero is for an Omniscient / Liberated One).

For a bigot:

  • Passions are:
    • Strong,
    • Violent,
    • Long-lasting.
Effects of strong passions:
  • Mind becomes:
    • Disturbed,
    • Impure,
    • Fluctuating.
  • Because of that, he:
    • Cannot stay steady in study or meditation.
    • Overreacts to even small incidents.
Train example:
  • Rajdhani Express – fast train, stops only at major stations → reaches quickly.
  • Firozpur Janta – stops at many small stations → takes days.
  • If your mind:
    • Stops at every petty situation,
    • Remains upset for minutes, hours, or days,
  • Then your spiritual journey becomes like a slow local train, not a fast express.
Key line from Jain perspective:

“Loss of crores is fine, death is fine, hunger is fine – But passions are not fine.”

If this isn’t your inner belief, you’re not yet on the true path.

Signs that kaṣāyni upśāntatā is missing:
  • One incident and you go into:
    • Days of anger, sulking, hurt.
  • You cannot forgive, cannot soften, cannot let go.
  • You refuse to accept fault, refuse to apologise.
  • Ground becomes hard-hearted:
    • Wisdom is likened to a fruit tree,
    • But the soil is uncultivated, hard → nothing grows.

2) Antar vairāgya nahi

No inner detachment / internal non-attachment

Antar vairāgya =

  • Antar – inner
  • Vairāgya – detachment / non-attachment.

Not just outer renunciation (clothes, lifestyle), but:

A fading fascination for worldly objects/events from within.

For the bigot:

  • There is no internal detachment, so:
    • Mind constantly wanders outward.
    • During bhakti or study, attention runs to:
      • Shops, food, social media, people, plans, etc.
Reason?
  • Deep delusion: “Joy is outside – in worldly objects and events.”
  • As long as you secretly believe this, mind will rush outwards.
Signs of antar vairāgya arising:
  • The curiosity and excitement for:
    • Movies, parties, restaurants, gossip, etc.
    • Slowly fades (not instantly vanishes, but fades).
  • You may still:
    • Attend events, join outings, go to dinners,
    • But the inner buzz is much less.
  • Phrases like “delight / thrill / excitement” start shifting:
    • From worldly activities
    • To bhakti, satsang, contemplation.
Politeness vs reality example:
  • You go to someone’s party and say: “It was fantastic, I loved it!” (to encourage them) But inside you know the joy you feel in satsang is on a different level.
  • Over time, this mismatch leads to:
    • Feeling “I’m almost cheating the world – I don’t actually get joy from what they get joy from.”
    • That inner feeling can ripen into desire for renunciation (dikshā):

“If I’m anyway not attached inside, maybe I should fully renounce outwardly too.”

For a bigot, however:

  • Inner attraction to world has not faded.
  • He’s religiously active but still deeply pulled by external pleasures.
  • Hence: “Antar vairāgya nahi”.

3) Saralpanu nahi

No simplicity / straightforwardness

Saralpanu here does not mean:

  • Simple dressing, basic food, minimal possessions (that’s under antar vairāgya).

Here it means:

Straightforwardness & frankness in seeing and owning your faults.

True saralpanu:
  • Admitting mistakes quickly, without:
    • Long justifications,
    • Emotional drama,
    • Needing to be caught “red-handed.”
  • Focus is on:

“What did I do that contributed to this?” Rather than: “Why are they blaming me when I’m innocent?”

Coin example:
  • A fake coin looks similar to a real coin, that’s why it “passes” as real.
  • If someone blames you:
    • There must be some similarity between what they accuse and something in you (gesture, tone, history),
    • Otherwise, no blame would “stick” at all.
  • So a true seeker thinks:

“Why didn’t they feel like blaming Lord Mahavira or Mallinath? Why me? What in me allowed this perception?”

Even if:

  • Exact words of the blame are wrong or exaggerated,
  • The seeker keeps focus on:

“What did I supply that made this believable?”

A bigot, with no saralpanu, does the opposite:

  • Immediately defends:
    • “I didn’t say that.”
    • “I didn’t mean it.”
    • “They misunderstood.”
  • Doesn’t look at even the 1% of truth in the accusation.
  • Just wants to prove total innocence.

Saralpanu is tied to:

  • Soft-heartedness,
  • Deep interest in inner purification,
  • Willingness to see oneself as fallible.

Without it:

  • You can’t focus within; you’re too busy defending and justifying.

4) Madhyasthata nahi

No neutrality / impartiality

Madhyasthata =

  • Neutrality, impartiality, balance in:
    • Thinking,
    • Evaluating,
    • Responding.

It comes from:

An open mind, not from merely taking a vow “I’ll be impartial”.

Why open-mindedness is crucial:
  • Life constantly demands:
    • Judgments (What is this? What happened?)
    • Decisions (Should we do A or B?)
    • Evaluations (Is this right, appropriate, timely?).
  • So “I won’t be judgmental at all” is not practical.
    • The question is: On what basis am I judging?
Right way:
  • Judge dispassionately, not from:
    • Ego,
    • Attachment,
    • Group bias,
    • Habitual preferences.
Examples used:

1. Training Nemi:

  • Earlier, he would ask, “Bapa, what would YOU choose?” and simply copy.
  • Now, he’s told: “First you think and answer. I want to see your discernment and impartial view.”
  • Point: You will not always be physically with Guru; you need internal alignment + independent clarity.

2. Charity example (temple vs sādharamik bhakti):

  • Building a marble temple is great.
  • But in COVID times, the “need of the hour” may be:
    • Feeding the hungry,
    • Supporting those in distress (sādharamik bhakti).
  • A neutral, open mind can see:
    • In one time period, temple charity may be priority.
    • In another, humanitarian service may be Lord’s wish.
  • A closed mind insists:

“Temple only! Let people suffer; the marble must be finished.”

3. Other religions:

  • Closed mind: “Vaishnavs are like this, Muslims are like that” → constant criticism.
  • Neutral mind:
    • Sees what’s beautiful in each:
      • Vaishnavism’s bhakti of love,
      • Islam’s discipline, devotion, submission.
    • Moves from tolerance → acceptance → respect.
Bigot’s pattern:
  • Very self-opinionated:
    • “Whatever I feel is right is the truth.”
  • Stubborn thinking:
    • Refuses to reconsider when presented with new understanding (even by Sadguru).
  • Over-attached to own view, so:
    • Cannot receive correction or different viewpoints.
    • Remains stuck; cannot march on the path of liberation.

6. The inner picture: the bigot as a “straw in the wind”

Because he lacks all four:

1. Passions weakened? – No.

2. Inner detachment? – No.

3. Simplicity/straightforwardness? – No.

4. Impartiality? – No.

His inner state:

  • Little direction,
  • Even less control,
  • So he’s like:

“A piece of straw in a gust of wind.”

  • Situations (karma, people, moods) simply blow him around.
  • His religious activities:
    • Many externally,
    • But futile for Self-realisation, because:
      • They don’t transform heart, mind, or vision.

Thus:

  • He is religious but not spiritual.
  • A hypocrite in the precise sense:
    • Big show outside,
    • Empty of the four essential virtues inside.

7. How this verse meets you (practical self-check)

Param Krupalu Dev is basically saying:

“You may or may not relate to earlier verses (fasts, big rituals, etc.), but this verse everyone must check.”

Because:

  • You might not be doing:
    • Long fasts,
    • Big rituals,
    • Temple sevas.
  • But here the question is:
    • Are these four virtues arising in you or not?

If no, then:

  • You may be around religion, but
  • You are still on the side of matārthī (bigot).

If yes (even partially, increasingly):

  • You are moving towards being an ātmārthī (true seeker).

So the verse acts as a mirror, asking:

1. Are my passions slowly weakening, or as strong as ever?

2. Is there a fading of attraction to worldly pleasures, or same (or more) excitement?

3. Do I admit faults quickly, or always defend/justify?

4. Can I listen with openness, or am I fixed in “my way is the only way”?

8. One-line summary

A person can have the right God, Guru, scriptures, rituals, and even high philosophy – but if there is no subsiding of passions, no inner detachment, no simplicity, and no impartiality, he is a matārthī, an unfortunate bigot, unworthy yet of the true path, like a straw in the wind – religiously busy, but spiritually nowhere.
