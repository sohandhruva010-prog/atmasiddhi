Absolutely. This satsang continues directly from “Aho! Aho! Shri Sadguru.” In the previous satsang, the disciple realized the immeasurable upkar (benevolence) of his Sadguru. Now a natural question arises:

“If my Guru has done so much for me, how can I possibly repay Him?”

The verse Gurudev explains is:

“Shu Prabhucharan kaney dharu? Atmathi sau heen;
Te to Prabhue apiyo, Vartu charanadheen.”

Essential meaning:

What can I possibly offer at Your Lotus Feet? Everything is inferior to the soul. And the awareness/realisation of that very soul was given to me by You. Therefore, I resolve to spend my life under Your command.

And that last movement is the heart of this satsang:

He begins by searching for a gift to give the Guru. He ends by giving the Guru himself.

Atmasiddhi — “Shu Prabhucharan Kaney Dharu?”

1. From gratitude comes the desire to repay

The previous verse was:

“Aho! Aho! Upkar!”

The disciple was overwhelmed by what the Guru had done for him.

Now that gratitude naturally develops into:

“What can I do in return?”

Gurudev explains that when someone genuinely recognizes a favour, an ethical person naturally wants to reciprocate it.

Not because the other person demanded repayment.

But because gratitude itself says:

“You've done so much for me. What can I do for You?”

So the disciple isn't asking Gurudev:

“Tell me what gift You want.”

He is asking himself:

“Shu Prabhucharan kaney dharu?” What can I possibly place at His Feet?

2. The disciple starts thinking of actual gifts 😂

Gurudev humorously takes us through his thought process.

Maybe:

  • sweets?
  • coconut sweet?
  • fruit?
  • mangoes?
  • Swarovski crystal?
  • clothes?
  • shoes?
  • a nice pen?
  • watch?
  • spectacles?
  • gold?
  • even the Koh-i-Noor diamond?

The disciple genuinely wants to offer something precious.

But then there is a problem.

His Guru has taught him the glory of the soul.

So every time he thinks of something, he compares it to the soul.

And immediately:

Too small.

Diamond?

Too small.

Gold?

Too small.

Koh-i-Noor?

Still insignificant.

Because:

“Atmathi sau heen.”

Everything is inferior to the soul.

3. Why does EVERYTHING suddenly seem insignificant?

This is deeper than saying material things are bad.

The disciple's value system has fundamentally changed.

His Guru didn't merely give him information about the soul.

Gurudev says the Guru:

“sang the glory of the soul.”

The disciple has absorbed that glory so deeply that the soul has become his standard of value.

So whenever he evaluates something:

“How valuable is this compared with the Self?”

Everything collapses in comparison.

Even a diamond becomes trivial.

That's an important transformation:

Before Self-realisation:
Material object → “Wow, precious!”
After understanding the glory of the soul:

Material object compared to soul → insignificant.

His definition of “precious” has changed.

4. “But then I'll offer the most precious thing — the soul!”

The disciple reaches the obvious conclusion.

If everything else is inferior to the soul, then perhaps:

“I'll offer the soul itself.”

Problem.

“Te to Prabhue apiyo.”
That itself was given to me by You!

Gurudev clarifies this carefully.

The Guru did not literally create or give the disciple his soul.

The soul already existed.

What He gave him was:

awareness of the soul
Self-realisation
Self-awareness

Before meeting Guru, the disciple's belief was essentially:

“I am this body.”

Guru destroyed that ignorance and revealed:

“You are the soul.”

So experientially the disciple feels:

“You gave me my Self.”

5. Gurudev's regifting example 😂

Now there's another problem.

Suppose someone gave you a gift last year.

You forget who gave it.

Later, you wrap it up…

…and accidentally give it back to the same person.

They say:

“Uh… I gave you this last year.”

Embarrassing 😂

Gurudev uses that analogy here.

The disciple thinks:

The most precious thing I have is awareness of my soul.

But:

Who gave me that awareness?

You did.

So how can I offer Your own gift back to You as repayment?

That would hardly repay anything.

6. So the disciple reaches a crisis

Think about his position:

Material things?

Too insignificant.

The soul/Self-realisation?

That itself was given by Guru.

So what remains?

This is where the verse transforms.

The disciple stops asking:

“What OBJECT can I give You?”

and realizes:

“I can give YOU my LIFE.”

Not physically.

Through obedience and surrender.

Hence:

“Vartu charanadheen.”

Let me live under Your command.

That becomes his Gurudakshina.

7. The entire verse moves from “something” to “myself”

Gurudev summarizes this beautifully.

The disciple's journey starts:

“I'll offer some mangoes.”

Then:

maybe chocolates…

maybe shoes…

maybe clothes…

maybe something expensive…

Eventually:

Everything gets rejected.

And Gurudev says:

He wanted to offer an object.

Finally, he offered himself.

That's the whole verse.

And Gurudev emphasizes that even offering himself feels insignificant compared to what he has received.

But it is the only thing left to offer.

8. Gurudev reconstructs the disciple's entire journey

Gurudev then beautifully imagines the disciple looking backward.

He remembers:

“I was lost.”

And:

“You held my hand.”
“You guided my steps.”
“When I was lonely, You befriended me.”
“When I was confused and deluded, You cleared my vision.”
“I was trapped in body-consciousness.”
“You destroyed the veil of ignorance.”
“You showed me the splendour of my soul.”
“You sang the glory of my soul.”

And finally:

“You gave me Self-awareness.”

Now imagine the disciple asking:

What gift could possibly equal THAT?

A gold pen?

Gold spectacles?

Some expensive object?

Gurudev almost laughs at the comparison.

The Guru took me from delusion to Self-realisation, and I'm going to repay Him with a pen?

The scales don't match.

9. Ideal Guru + Ideal Disciple

Gurudev then describes the beautiful relationship between an ideal Guru and ideal disciple.

The ideal Guru:

Wants nothing from the disciple.

Completely selfless.

No expectation of payment or return.

The ideal disciple:

Wants to give everything to the Guru.

So there is a beautiful paradox:

Guru wants nothing.

Disciple wants to give everything.

Gurudev connects this to Kabirji's description of the ideal Guru-disciple relationship.

That makes the disciple's surrender even more meaningful.

He's not surrendering because Guru demanded:

“Give me your life.”

Quite the opposite.

Guru wants nothing.

Gratitude makes the disciple want to give himself.

10. Gurudakshina — what does it actually mean?

Gurudev then explains the traditional concept of Gurudakshina.

In ancient Gurukul tradition, a disciple would:

  • go where the Guru lived,
  • serve Him,
  • learn from Him,
  • and when the period of learning ended, offer Gurudakshina.

Gurudev stresses:

Dakshina ≠ charity.

Charity is something you give.

Dakshina comes with the feeling:

“I have received something from You, and I wish to acknowledge that debt.”

Traditionally this is strongly associated with Gurupurnima.

But this disciple's Gurudakshina cannot simply be an object.

His Guru gave him Self-realisation.

What object could equal that?

11. Eklavya's Gurudakshina

Gurudev brings up the famous story of Eklavya and Dronacharya.

When Eklavya was asked for his thumb as Gurudakshina:

He didn't deliberate endlessly.

Gurudev summarizes his response almost as:

Heard → no thinking → executed.

Gurudev deliberately says he is discussing this from Eklavya's side—his readiness to give—not endorsing or fully judging Dronacharya's action.

In fact, Gurudev explicitly warns against casually labeling historical figures when we don't understand all their circumstances.

The relevant quality is:

The disciple's readiness to give.

12. Don't forget even tiny favours

This becomes a practical teaching beyond the Guru-disciple relationship.

Gurudev says an ethical person does not forget any favour, even something as small as someone helping remove a thorn from your foot.

You should naturally search for an opportunity to reciprocate.

He gives the everyday example of people working in the kitchen.

Don't think:

“That's their job. They're supposed to do it.”

If someone makes something nice:

thank them.

Appreciate them.

If you feel like giving something, give.

Gurudev calls this basic ethics.

And then his point becomes:

If we shouldn't forget even a tiny worldly favour…

how could we possibly forget the Guru's favour?

13. You may NEVER repay Guru — but you should WANT to This distinction is really important.

Gurudev isn't saying:

“You must successfully repay your Guru.”

He explicitly says:

You can't.

You will never truly equal what has been given.

But that doesn't mean:

“Oh well, Guru is compassionate. He doesn't want anything anyway.”

Gurudev rejects that attitude.

There should be a genuine:

desire to repay

and even an

urgency to repay.

The impossibility of repayment should increase gratitude—not create complacency.

14. “Prabhucharan” — not merely Guru anymore

Like the previous satsang, Gurudev notices the disciple's language.

He doesn't say:

“What can I give Guru?”

He says:

“Prabhucharan”

The Feet of the Lord.

Gurudev says:

“Guru is gone now! Now he sees God only — Prabhu.”

Meaning the disciple's reverence has reached the point where Guru and Prabhu aren't separate in his devotional experience.

This continues directly from the previous verse:

Sadguru → Prabhu.

15. Why does he offer it at Guru's FEET?

Gurudev then focuses on another tiny word.

The disciple doesn't say:

“What can I place in Your hands?”

He asks:

“What can I place at Your Feet?”

In ordinary life, when you give someone a present, you hand it to them.

But Gurudev says the disciple's relationship with Guru isn't an ordinary social transaction.

Placing something at the Lotus Feet represents:

  • humility,
  • reverence,
  • surrender,
  • recognition of divinity.

Gurudev even jokes about how when we donate money, we sometimes want to physically put the envelope into Guru's hand so:

“He should register it.” 😂

Because somewhere inside:

I want Him to know that I gave this.

That's ego.

True offering doesn't need recognition.

The disciple's feeling is simply:

Place it at His Feet.

16. Even the word “kaney” has significance

Gurudev briefly goes into something quite subtle.

“Kaney” means roughly near/by:

“What can I place near Your Feet?”

Gurudev recalls his PhD guide, Dr. Ramanlal C. Shah, pointing out that “kaney” is characteristic of the Charotar region, where Atmasiddhi was composed.

Gurudev finds this beautiful because even the local linguistic flavour of the place becomes woven into the scripture.

He interprets it almost as Param Krupalu Dev acknowledging a debt toward the place where the composition arose.

And Gurudev specifically says this is more of a heart-to-heart observation, not something intended for the quiz/book.

17. Why these four “Aho Aho” verses matter so much

Gurudev talks about Laghuraj Swami / Lalluji Muni.

The impact of these verses was apparently so strong that he felt:

These cannot merely be recited whenever we happen to recite the entire Atmasiddhi.

They should be sung twice daily.

These four verses became part of Devvandan.

Gurudev himself places enormous importance on them.

He says that even when dealing with someone's final moments, he first tries to establish:

refuge.

Then:

  • awareness of the soul,
  • distinction between body and soul,
  • glory of the soul,
  • renunciation.

But first:

Seek refuge.

So these aren't merely sentimental verses about loving Guru.

They capture the final emotional state produced by the entire spiritual journey.

18. The two things the disciple has discovered

Gurudev again repeats the beautiful point from the previous satsang.

Through this journey the disciple realizes two things:

1. His pure Self

and

2. The immense benevolence of his Sadguru.

The first gives him Self-realisation.

The second gives him gratitude, refuge and surrender.

And now the two come together:

The Guru gave me awareness of the most precious thing—my Self. Therefore, what can possibly be precious enough to offer Him in return?

Answer:

Nothing.

So:

I offer my life.

19. “Vartu Charanadheen” — this is the real offering This is the culmination of the satsang.

Vartu = let me live / conduct myself
Charanadheen = under Your Feet / under Your command

Gurudev explains it as:

“I firmly resolve to live at Your command for the rest of my life.”

Not:

“I'll obey You when Your command matches what I already wanted.”

But:

Let my life match Your ajna.

Gurudev makes a funny reference to people matching clothes:

Everything matching!

But then says:

Match yourself with the command of the Enlightened One.

Your:

  • choices,
  • conduct,
  • actions,
  • direction,

should increasingly become aligned with Guru's ajna.

20. And this begins “servitude”

Remember the structure Gurudev gave earlier:

The final four verses contain:

2 verses of gratitude

followed by 2 verses of servitude.

This verse is the bridge.

Gratitude has become so intense that it turns into:

surrender.

The disciple is essentially saying:

I cannot repay You with something I possess.

So let what I possess—including this life—operate according to You.

Gurudev says this acceptance of servitude will be expressed even more beautifully in the next verse.

The progression across these satsangs

I think these recent satsangs make much more sense when we connect them rather than treating each verse separately.

First: “Moksh kahyo nijshuddhata”

The disciple realizes:

My goal is complete purity of my own soul.

↓

Then: “Aho! Aho! Shri Sadguru”

After Self-realisation, he looks at Guru and realizes:

You brought me here.

That produces overwhelming gratitude.

↓

Now: “Shu Prabhucharan kaney dharu?”

Gratitude becomes:

How can I repay You?

He searches for something valuable enough.

↓

Fruit? Too small.

Gold? Too small.

Diamond? Too small.

Koh-i-Noor? Too small.

↓

Soul?

That's the most precious thing!

But then:

“Te to Prabhue apiyo.”

You gave me awareness of that very soul.

↓

So finally:

“Vartu Charanadheen.”

Then let me give You myself.

Not merely through an object.

Not merely through a donation.

Not merely through a gift on Gurupurnima.

But by saying:

Let the way I live become my offering.

The biggest practical takeaway The deepest part for me is that Gurudev changes the meaning of repaying Guru.

At first we naturally think:

“What can I do for BAPA?”

Maybe seva.

Maybe a physical offering.

Maybe some project.

Maybe something special for Him.

All of those can certainly be expressions of devotion.

But this verse keeps pushing deeper:

What would He actually value?

If the Guru's only motive is my spiritual welfare and liberation, then the greatest response to His upkar isn't merely giving Him something.

It is allowing what He has given me to actually transform how I live.

So, for example, if BAPA teaches me to remain happy, and I give Him a beautiful gift but continue choosing misery over every small situation—what have I really offered?

If He teaches me sincerity, and I do seva but remain insincere internally—what have I offered?

If He teaches me to reduce ego, but I want Him to notice how much seva I did—Gurudev's envelope example becomes very relevant.

If He teaches me to remember the goal, but my entire life continues revolving around everything except the goal—then the physical offering cannot substitute for charanadheenta.

The verse therefore takes Guru-bhakti from emotion into conduct.

Yesterday:

“Aho! Aho!”

I feel Your upkar.

Today:

“Vartu Charanadheen.”

Now let my life reflect that gratitude.

And I think that is the key transition:

Gratitude → desire to repay → inability to repay → surrender → obedience.

The disciple realizes:

“Nothing I own is worthy of You. So instead of merely giving You something I have, let me give You the way I live.”

That is his Gurudakshina.
