2) Part A — Revision: 5 ways to steady the mind (applied to anger)

These five methods were given in an earlier session (reference: Patrank-925). They were introduced as general tools to steady the mind against negative thoughts. Here they are applied specifically to anger. Each method belongs to one of three "modes" — doing, thinking, or witnessing.

Method 1: Substitute

Core idea: Replace the wrong feeling with the right feeling. Here — replace anger with forgiveness. Don't wait for the feeling to pass. Actively substitute it.

What this looks like: When you feel anger toward someone, consciously bring up the feeling of forgiveness. This is not suppression. It is a deliberate replacement of one mental state with another.

Verse connection: This is the simplest and most "doing-oriented" method. Guru says the path of liberation is as simple as this — "substitute anger by forgiveness, etc." — so brief, so clear.

Method 2: Cause

Core idea: Find the real, primary cause of the situation — specifically, your own contribution to it. We habitually focus only on the other person's role. The teaching says: find yours too.

The key shift: Guru says your contribution is always described as "a bit" — because we always find we were only a little bit at fault. But finding even that small contribution is enough to change the internal state.

What rises when you find your role:
  • "I won't do it next time" — not anger, not blame. A quiet resolution.
  • Forgiveness comes effortlessly: "I was partly at fault too, so I'd rather forgive you — because if I send you to hell, I'll have to go there too."
What rises when you don't find your role:
  • Anger — because if the other person is 100% responsible for the mess, there is nothing to temper the rage.

The mechanism: Anger rises because we focus on the secondary cause (what the other person did). Finding the primary cause (your own contribution) removes the fuel. Forgiveness then arises not from effort but from understanding.

Method 3: Consequences

Core idea: Think through what will happen if you react. Consider the karmic bondage, the strained relationships, the worldly harm. Is venting the anger worth the cost?

Example given — the lying boss: Your boss says something that is a complete lie. Can you say, "No Sir, you are lying"? You feel it is better to remain quiet — because the harm of reacting (losing your job, damaging the relationship permanently) is far greater than the discomfort of staying calm. So you calculate, and you choose silence.

Important nuance on feelings vs behaviour: Guru is careful here. He says your internal feelings may still change — you may still feel angry inside, because that is your past instinct arising before you can even catch it. That is okay. But your behaviour and words must remain steady. The resolve is: "However much my feelings change, my thoughts change — my behaviour will not change. I will not react."

This creates space for the impure feelings to subside on their own.

The Gurdjieff story: When Gurdjieff's father was dying, Gurdjieff asked for his last piece of advice. His father said: "Whenever you want to react — resolve to react after 24 hours." Not: don't react. But: react tomorrow, at exactly this time. By then, you may find it unimportant. By then, you may even see your own role in the situation. The delay is the practice.

Method 4: Distance from the trigger

Core idea: When you cannot manage the situation internally, change it externally. Either send the triggering person away, or remove yourself from the space — even briefly.

The vessel on the stove analogy: Something is boiling in a vessel on the stove. If you want to stop the boiling, you have two options: (a) switch off the heat, or (b) lift the vessel off the stove. Either way, it cools. The trigger is the stove. You are the vessel. Step away from the heat.

Practical example: "Just for a while, go to the Community Garden for a stroll. After three rounds in the fresh air, the healthy body and healthy mind will have reset. Sometimes the solution is so simple. Just go a little far from your trigger."

Important distinction: This is called "spiritual distancing." The ideal is to do the whole process mentally — without changing anything externally. But if you cannot manage it internally, change your external situation. The point in either case: do not react impulsively.

Method 5: Dominate (Witnessing mode)

Core idea: If you have the capacity, skip analysis entirely. The moment you detect anger, go directly into the witnessing mode. Do not think about the cause. Do not think about the consequences. Simply observe the feeling as a witness.

The internal statement: "I am only the witness. I am just a witness."

Why it is the best: It bypasses the thinking and doing modes entirely. No analysis, no substitution — just pure observation. The feeling loses its grip because there is no identification with it.

Why it is the hardest: Guru acknowledges that in times of anger, most people do not have the capacity to go directly into witnessing mode. That is precisely why the other four methods exist. They are for the moments when witnessing is not accessible.

The three modes summarised:
Mode Methods

Doing mode Substitute (replace anger with forgiveness) · Distance yourself from the trigger

Thinking mode Cause (find the real cause) · Consequences (weigh the harm of reacting)

Witnessing Dominate — directly observe without thinking or doing. The highest mode capacity.

The Paresha / Pareshaan story — witnessing in daily life

Guru gave a "command" to the president of Shrimad Rajchandra Mission Dharampur USA: start calling his wife Paresha by the name "Pareshaan" (meaning troubled/disturbed). The wife was instructed to take it positively: "Pare hu" (I am beyond) + "shaan se" (with pride and grace) = "I am beyond circumstances, with grace."

What this teaches: Do not take anything personally — not "I love you," not "I hate you." Sideline the ego. An active ego swings between attachment (when praised) and aversion (when criticised). Go into the witnessing mode. Hold onto the supreme truth: he is a pure soul, and so am I. That is the only truth. Everything else — the lovely phases and the menacing phases — are not truth.

3) Part B — Argument 2: the disciple's confusion about the plurality of sects

The disciple's second doubt (verse 93)

"There are many sects and schools of thought in the world, each expounding different paths to attain liberation. It is impossible to determine which of them is correct. Having studied the beliefs of various religions and philosophies, I am confused."

Why Guru places this as the second argument, not the first

The existence of many paths is not itself a reason for confusion — Guru makes this clear. The disciple is confused not because of the diversity, but because he lacks a Sadguru. After the Guru's resolution, the diversity will remain exactly as it is — but the confusion will be gone. "Diversity is not the problem. The missing ingredient is the Sadguru."

4) Understanding the depth of the confusion — the hierarchy within one religion

To make us truly feel what the disciple experiences, Guru walks through the actual layers of division within a single religion, Jainism:

1. Dharma (religion): Jainism, Buddhism, Sikhism, Hinduism, Christianity, and so on.

2. Sect (Sampradaay): Within Jainism — Shwetambar or Digambar.

3. Sub-sect: Within Shwetambar — Sthanakvasi (do not visit temples) or Deravasi (visit temples).

4. Monastic order (Gacch): Within Deravasi — Tapagacch, Khartargacch, Achalgacch, and others.

5. Group (Samudaay): Within Tapagacch alone — 20 different groups, each with multiple acharyas.

6. Personal preference: Even within one samudaay, one may prefer one particular acharya over another.

Why Guru goes through this: Not to mock the system, but to validate the disciple's sense of overwhelm. The proliferation of choices is genuinely vast. "I'm not saying anyone is wrong or right, good or bad. I am saying — how do I select?" And Guru adds: last year, a 20th samudaay was formed within Tapagacch, within Deravasi Shwetambar Jain. The layers keep multiplying.

The real point: This is not about finding a problem with diversity. It is about understanding why the disciple, without a Sadguru, feels paralysed.

5) The samayik example — same practice, three mutually contradictory external forms

Samayik is a 48-minute practice of equanimity, observed across all Jain sub-sects. But look at how the three major sub-sects actually practise it:

Sthanakvasi: Must wear the muhapatti (a small cloth for covering the mouth) tied to the ears for the full 48 minutes. It must be worn — not just held.

Deravasi: Must hold the muhapatti in the hand and bring it up to cover the mouth whenever speaking. It must be held — not worn permanently.

Digambar: Must not use any muhapatti at all. It is a possession, and possessions introduce violence — the concern of washing it, cleaning it, caring for it. The monk is unclothed, so a cloth in the hand is even more out of place. Better to focus entirely on the absolute path.

So now: how do I do samayik? Some say wear it. Some say hold it. Some say touch nothing. Each position has an internal logic and Guru presents each sympathetically. He even adds a comic observation: during Covid, designer masks appeared — Holi masks, Diwali masks, masks in the colour of one's outfit — illustrating how easily we can get completely absorbed in the external form and lose the inner purpose entirely.

What the Enlightened Master says: "Samayik is not a set of equipment and rituals. It is practising a state of equanimity for 48 minutes. Give me that. Manage the external yourself. I am not telling you to change your sect. If you were born in a Sthanakvasi family, wear it on your ears. If Deravasi, hold it in your hand. The external is your business. Give me the inner state."

The lesson: Once you lose sight of the internal, absolute purpose, you start clinging to external method. You begin insisting that your method is the only valid one. You will "strive relentlessly to prove that liberation can be attained only by the method propounded by the sect I have accepted." This is where the confusion compounds.

6) Verse 105 — the Guru's answer

Key terms of the verse

  • Chhodi — give up, leave, abandon (Guru says: "I'm not using high words like 'renounce.' Just: give up.")
  • Mat darshan tano — regarding your beliefs and philosophies (mat = opinions/beliefs; darshan = schools of thought/philosophy)
  • Agrah — insistence, strong rigid opinions
  • Vikalp — choices, prejudices, strong preferences
  • Kahyo marg — the path indicated in the previous verses (verses 98–104)
  • Aa sadhashe — if you follow/pursue it
  • Janma tehna alp — your future births will be very few

The verse in plain language

"Give up, regarding your beliefs and philosophies, two things: (1) insistence and (2) strong choices/prejudices. Pursue the path indicated previously. You will not take infinite time. Your future births will be very few."

7) The two things to give up — agrah and vikalp explained

Agrah — insistence

Holding a strong, rigid opinion that your path is the only correct path and all others are wrong. All your energy goes into proving this. Even what you call "satsang" may really be an attempt to establish the supremacy of your position.

Agrah carries heavy attachment to your path and heavy aversion to others'. Both passions are present but aversion is dominant.

Example: "Liberation is possible only through Jainism." Param Krupalu Dev was Jain himself — and chose Jainism freely, not because of family tradition. He found it perfect and followed it wholeheartedly. And yet He writes: even this insistence — liberation only through Jainism — was dropped by this soul a long time ago. Because His focus was on the absolute path of liberation, not on the external method.

Example: "A Guru is needed for liberation. If you do not have a Guru, you can never attain liberation." This may be your experience and your conviction — and you have adopted it for yourself. But when you begin insisting it applies to everyone — that becomes agrah.

Vikalp — choices / prejudices

A strong preference for a particular viewpoint or method, to the extent that you consider it the only thing worth adopting. Milder than agrah — more attachment, less aversion — but still binding.

Example: "I only meditate. I don't do devotional practices. Devotion is not my path." Or conversely: "Devotion is everything. Someone who only meditates and does not perform devotion on Guru Purnima will never attain liberation."

What Param Krupalu Dev says: If your attachment, aversion, and ignorance are reducing — and if you are awakening to the pure Soul and staying in that awareness — then you are on the right path. Full stop. He has no interest in which method you used to get there.

The stubborn child analogy

A child says: "Mummy, I want mango juice — and if I don't get it, I won't eat anything." Your choice may be perfectly reasonable. But the strength of the choice is the problem. With choices that strong, you start doubting others, arguing with others — and instead of your transmigration decreasing, it increases. In the name of religion.

The combined harm

Both agrah and vikalp trap the soul in attachment and aversion — ironically in the name of religion. The very pursuit that is meant to reduce passions ends up intensifying them. "Religion does not begin without you shedding your insistence and choices."

8) Absolute path vs relative path — the crucial distinction

This is the conceptual backbone of verse 105.

Absolute path of liberation (parmarth mokshmarg): Reducing attachment, aversion, and ignorance. Awakening to the pure Soul. Staying in the awareness of the pure Soul. This is undisputed and universal — applicable across all religions, sects, and methods.

Relative path of liberation (vyavhar mokshmarg): External rituals, methods, equipment, sectarian rules, practices. These are fine — the body needs something to do, so external disciplines are inevitable. But they are the means, not the end.

The error: When the soul loses sight of the absolute path, it begins clinging to the relative path. And then it insists: this external method — my method — is the only valid means of liberation.

Guru's instruction: "I am not saying give up the relative path — then you'll become a lazy bum. Something or the other must be done. I am saying: give up the strong insistence and choices regarding the relative path. And simply pursue the absolute path as stated in verses 98–104."

9) What the result is — janma tehna alp (very few future births)

If you give up agrah and vikalp and pursue the absolute path:

  • If efforts are right and intense: Liberation in this very birth. Siddhashila at the end of this birth.
  • If efforts are right but somewhat weak (genuine faith, but with laziness, frequent slipping into unawareness, excessive negligence): Very few future births — at most 15, possibly as few as 3.

Why the phrase "very few" (alp) matters: The word "infinite" had been appearing repeatedly in earlier verses to describe the span of transmigration. Now, the Guru replaces "infinite" with "very few." The contrast is the teaching: even a small but sincere shift — giving up insistence, pursuing the absolute — compresses infinite wandering into a handful of lifetimes.

Connection to the introduction of Atmasiddhi: The introduction says: if one "strictly obeys the guidance of the Sadguru" — meaning gives up self-willed behaviour, stubbornness, sticking to your own viewpoint — one attains Samyak Darshan (right faith). With right faith, wrong faith is destroyed. With right faith deepened, likes and dislikes are gradually and completely destroyed. With their complete destruction — liberation. The chain: agrah/vikalp dropped → right faith → passions reduced → liberation.

10) Unity in diversity — how the Guru resolves the confusion

The disciple could not see unity. The Guru shows him it is there.

Guru shares that from ages 17–18, he spent 10–11 months going every day from 3 to 6 pm to Chetna bookstore, opposite the University of Bombay. The owner let him read for free — the books cost ₹3,000–4,000 and he could not afford them. He read J. Krishnamurti, Osho, and many others. He was not confused. He could see unity in the diversity.

The bread analogy

Bread, rotli, bhakhri, puri — different words, different forms, different textures. But all made of wheat. The substance is one; the forms are many. Similarly, different teachers use different language for the same truth. J. Krishnamurti called it "choiceless, effortless awareness." The discourse at hand calls it "witnessing mode." Hearing it expressed in a new form makes you feel you have encountered something fresh and profound — but you are hearing the same truth again.

Purna and shunya — completeness and voidness

Vedanta describes the soul as purna (complete, full). Buddhism describes the nature of phenomena as shunya (void, empty). These sound like opposites — and if you are committed to one viewpoint, the other seems like a contradiction. But anekantvada (the Jain doctrine of multiplicity of viewpoints) resolves it: "The soul is complete by nature and devoid of impurities." Purna and shunya are two sides of the same coin — one seen from one angle, the other from another. They are not in conflict.

The carnival / funfair analogy

At a funfair or carnival, there are different games, different foods — sev puri, Mexican food, many options. Nobody stands in front of the carnival saying "I am confused — I cannot determine which stall is correct." They enjoy the variety. Similarly: different bhajans, different mantras, different ways of performing puja, different venues for satsang — the change in location (Community Garden → Yajnasthal → somewhere else when the rains come), the change in chants (Krupalu Deva → Krupalu Dev) — all of this should add a celebratory, carnival dimension to the spiritual path, not cause confusion or complaint.

Why spirituality benefits from variety: Guru says, "Spirituality is quite difficult and can feel dull — because you have to cut your sensual pleasures. That is dull in a certain sense. Different forms of practice keep the path alive. And if you are a true seeker, it becomes the most interesting path of all."

11) The real test — not which sect, but what is the inner state?

Param Krupalu Dev's only question to any practitioner is:

  • What is the state of your attachment? Is it reducing?
  • What is the state of your aversion? Is it reducing?
  • What is your awareness of the truth you have accepted? Do you live in that awareness?

Whether there is a murti of Thakurji (Krishna) or a cross of Christ in front of that person — if their internal focus is on how to reduce their passions, they are on the right path. This is the only measure.

12) Personal and institutional application — warnings given

Even your own mission can become a trap

Guru says to SRMD members directly: "Shrimad Rajchandra Mission is helping you — but it can also drown you." If you start thinking only of "our mission," "our followers," "our people being safe" — you have become sectarian. You must think of humanity. When you cling to the sect, insistence and choices creep in, and transmigration increases rather than decreasing.

The balance of service and spiritual pursuit

Mission statement: "Realise your true self — and serve." Note the word "and," not "then." Both together. But the balance must not be lost. "If you are giving six hours to service, give me six hours to spiritual pursuit also. If you have only six hours total, give three to each. Our main purpose in coming here is self-realisation."

Being a lover of truth vs a lover of your sect

You must be a lover of truth, not a lover of your sect. Narrow sectarianism traps you in attachment and aversion. You can only truly honour diversity when you see the unity beneath it. And once you see that all paths are pointing to the same absolute goal — reducing attachment, aversion, and ignorance, awakening to the pure Soul — the differences become enriching rather than confusing.

16) Practical application notes

A) Before reacting to anything — the 24-hour rule

Next time you feel the urge to react sharply (anger, frustration, correction), set a concrete time 24 hours later: "I will respond at 8:30 pm tomorrow." Notice what changes in how important it feels by then, and whether your sense of your own role in the situation shifts.

B) Find your contribution first

In any situation where you feel wronged, before focusing on the other person's role, write down: "What was my contribution — even a small one?" Notice how this changes the feeling that follows.

C) Identify your agrah and vikalp

Pick one area of your spiritual practice or religious belief. Ask honestly:

  • "Is this a conviction I hold — or an insistence I defend?"
  • "Am I open to the possibility that someone practising very differently could be on the same path?"

D) The absolute path as the daily measure

At the end of each day, instead of asking "Did I do my practices?" ask:

  • "Is my attachment reducing?"
  • "Is my aversion reducing?"
  • "Was my awareness of the truth stronger today than yesterday?"

E) Practise seeing unity in diversity

In the next satsang, event, or religious gathering that feels different from your norm, look for the one thing that is the same underneath the different forms. Name it to yourself.
