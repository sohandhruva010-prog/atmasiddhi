Verse 22 — “Hoy mumukshu jīva te samje e vichār; hoy matārthī jīva te avlo le nirdhār.”
One-line essence

This verse splits religious people into two kinds:

1. the mumukshu (genuinely liberation-seeking), who understands the Guru-taught path of reverence correctly; and

2. the matārthī (sectarian/fanatic), who misunderstands and draws wrong conclusions—often weaponizing partial truths.

1) Where Verse 22 sits in the flow

  • 9–18: the indispensability of a living Sadguru; inner enemies fall “with little effort.”
  • 19: Zenith of humility—even a disciple who attains omniscience keeps revering his Guru (who may still be chhadmasth).
  • 20: The Vītrāgs themselves teach the path of humility; only a few grasp its purpose (melting self-will).
  • 21: A hard warning: an asadguru who exploits devotion binds mahā-mohaniya karma and sinks in saṁsāra.
  • 22 (today): The decisive fork: will you be a mumukshu who reads all this right, or a matārthī who misreads it?

It’s the second-last verse of the introduction and the launchpad for the next section on matārthī-lakṣaṇ (traits of sectarianism).

2) Key vocabulary (as used here)

  • Asadguru: not Self-realised, yet claims to be Guru; can help worldly things, not spiritual welfare.
  • Mumukshu: one whose only aim is Self-realisation and liberation.
  • Matārthī: sectarian/bigot—attached to a sect’s viewpoint (“we believe…”) rather than truth. Near cousin of swachhand (attachment to my opinions), but here attachment is to the sect’s opinions.
  • Vichār: the teaching just delivered—Sadguru’s centrality, reverence, humility’s purpose.
  • Samje (correctly) vs avlo le nirdhār (draws opposite conclusions).
  • Sadguru-no āśay: the intent behind the Guru’s words—vital to grasp, not just the literal line.

3) The verse’s two halves, unpacked

A) “Hoy mumukshu jīva te samje e vichār”

A genuine seeker:

  • Aims: Self-realisation now, liberation ultimately.
  • Seeks a Sadguru with the five marks (Self-realisation; equanimity; living per past karma; unprecedented, awakening speech; highest scriptural insight).
  • Shows vinay (reverence) + obeys ājñā; uses humility to shred swachhand.
  • Understands correctly both words and intent—hears the point beneath the sentence.

Anecdote hint: kids/NRIs often say “I understood everything,” but a quick follow-up shows they missed the meaning (e.g., mistaking dosh—flaws—for dost—friend). The mumukshu doesn’t passively nod; he checks and verifies understanding.

B) “Hoy matārthī jīva te avlo le nirdhār”

A sectarian/fanatic:

  • Reverence is for “my sect’s belief”, not for truth.
  • Obstinate about his view; prejudiced toward others (teacher’s “Pride and Prejudice” quip).
  • Misreads scriptures/Guru because he doesn’t catch the intent—and then locks wrong inferences in place.
Two vivid misreadings:

1. Scripture says spirituality is inner, not in outer bodily acts → matārthī decides, “So I’ll drop all wholesome daily practices, bowing, pūjā, chanting.” (Wrong: the intent was to shift center inward, not to despise supportive disciplines.)

2. “Don’t stay in the company of asadgurus” → matārthī concludes, “I’ll avoid all Gurus, even Sadgurus.” He turns a surgical caution into a blanket rejection.

Result: he harms his spiritual welfare—often for a lifetime.

4) Organization isn’t the enemy—sectarianism is

  • Institutions (four-fold saṅgh) are ancient and useful.
  • But as the river Ganga gets muddy downstream, organizations can drift toward dry ritualism (jaḍ–śuṣka) as matārthīs dominate.
  • Hence the need for living Masters “yuge-yuge”—fresh revolutions of meaning cutting through accumulation of form.

6) Sectarian faith vs seeker’s faith (Bhaktāmbar example)

  • Matārthī recites Bhaktāmbar because “we were told” it “works”; never investigates meaning, author, context, aim.
  • Mumukshu asks: Who composed? Why begin with celestial devotees? What’s the import of each verse for me today? He converts tradition into living understanding.

7) Practical diagnostics (am I reading “right” or “wrong”?)

Signs you’re a mumukshu reading right
  • You keep asking: “What’s the Guru’s intent here?”
  • Vinay is making ātmā more central and swachhand thinner.
  • You seek attributes, not outfits; you’d choose a quiet householder-Sage over a celebrated robe if the former radiates Self-knowledge.
  • Rituals remain but become transparent—they aim your mind inward.
  • You’d drop worldly boons in a heartbeat for one clear ājñā that purifies ego.
Signs you’re slipping into matārthī misreading
  • “My sect/mission/numbers” quietly overshadow Self-realisation.
  • You quote scriptures to stop practice rather than purify it.
  • You avoid all Gurus “just in case”—or you follow any with robes “just because.”
  • You’re quick to judge; slow to inquire into meaning.
  • You want time/attention/blessings more than truth.

8) How to practice Verse 22 (today)

1. Intent-hunt: After any line you read/hear, ask: “What was the āśay (intent)?” Write one sentence.

2. Refuge check: List the five Sadguru marks; honestly score your current guide only on these.

3. Vinay audit: Did today’s reverence reduce self-will? If not, it was exercise, not vinay.

4. Ritual rethread: Keep the outer form you have—but explicitly tie it to an inner aim (e.g., 3 prostrations → “Je svarūp…Deh chhatā”—remind yourself why).

5. Company cleanse: Kindly reduce exposure to asadguru influences (even inherited ones) and increase Sadguru-saṅg.

9) Compact recap (one breath) Verse 22 draws the line: a mumukshu hears the Sadguru-taught path of reverence and gets it right—so he seeks the true Guru, obeys, melts self-will, and moves toward Self-realisation. A matārthī hears the same words and gets them wrong—clings to sect/banners, misapplies teachings, and blocks his own welfare. The whole next section will spell out the traits of the matārthī so you can stay a mumukshu.
