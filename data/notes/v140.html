Verse 140 of the Upsamhar, and it completes Param Krupalu Dev's description of Jnanidasha — the Enlightened State.

Verse 139 gave us the internal criterion:

“Mohbhaav kshay hoy jya, athva hoy prashant;
Te kahiye Jnanidasha, baki kahiye bhrant.”

Where delusion has been destroyed or deeply subsided, that is the Enlightened State.

Verse 140 now shows us the result of that inner transformation—how the entire world appears to such an Enlightened One:

“Sakal jagat te ethvat, athva swapna saman;
Te kahiye Jnanidasha, baki vachajnan.”

In essence:

The entire world appears like leftover food—utterly unattractive—or like a dream—fleeting and incapable of lasting satisfaction. That is the Enlightened State. Anything else is merely verbal knowledge. Pasted text(20261116-220628)

This verse is essentially a verification of Vairagya.

Section 12 — Upsamhar
Verse 140: How Does the World Appear to an Enlightened One?

Gurudev says there are only three main things to understand:

1. Sakal jagat te ethvat — the whole universe appears like leftover food.

2. Athva swapna saman — or like a dream.

3. Baki vachajnan — anything short of this is merely verbal knowledge. Pasted text(20261116-220628)

The first analogy explains the world's unattractiveness to a Jnani.

The second explains its transitoriness and inability to satisfy.

Together, they show the Jnani's extraordinary detachment.

The pure Self.

And Gurudev gives a simple psychological principle:

Once you've experienced what is most worthwhile, everything lesser begins appearing worthless. Pasted text(20261116-220628)

No.

The deepest practical takeaway

I think this verse gives us a very useful way of understanding Vairagya.

We often imagine detachment as:

“I want this, but I shouldn't have it.”

That's restraint.

Useful—but not the final state Gurudev is describing.

The Jnani's state is much deeper:

“Why would I want this?”

That's why leftover food is such a strong analogy.

If someone places half-eaten food in front of you, you don't require:

  • discipline,
  • a vow,
  • willpower,
  • meditation,

to stop yourself from eating it.

You simply:

don't find it attractive.

Similarly, you don't need to remind yourself every five minutes:

“Last night's dream car wasn't real happiness.”

You woke up.

The fascination naturally ended.

That's the transformation Param Krupalu Dev is pointing toward.

And for us, the practical question isn't:

“Does the whole world already look like leftover food to me?”

Clearly, Verse 140 is describing Jnanidasha, not asking a Mumukshu to pretend to have reached it.

A more useful question is:

Is my valuation beginning to move in that direction?

This pleasure is temporary.

This possession cannot satisfy me permanently.

This praise will pass.

This discomfort will pass too.

Getting one more thing will saturate one desire, not eliminate desire itself.

My happiness cannot safely be outsourced to changing objects.

Then the earlier definition of Vairagya becomes very powerful:

Detachment = non-dependency.

I can use things.

I can fulfill my role.

I can receive the effects of past karma.

I can appreciate what comes.

But I don't need to build my identity, pride or happiness upon it.

Because:

association does not have to become attachment. Pasted text(20261116-220628)
