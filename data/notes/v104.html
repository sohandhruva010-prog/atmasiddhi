This session is the opening framing for a long “Jñān Yajna” (study series) on Shri Atmasiddhi Shastra—presented for youngsters, in English, meant to work on three levels: academic, spiritual, emotional.

1) Session structure and what the speaker promises

Course size / commitment

  • Total planned: ~145 hours minimum
    • 142 verses + extra sessions + pujans + quizzes + exams
  • He asks people to write in rough for 3 days, understand his teaching style, then shift to “fair” notes.

4 topics he says he’ll cover “today”

1. Need to study Shri Atmasiddhi Shastra (this transcript mostly covers this)

2. Inspiration behind the composition (he says “just write: inspiration”)

3. Occasion: Aso Vad Ekam and Nadiad

4. Worthy receivers of the first copy of the Shastra

(He also previews tomorrow’s topics: title/name, subject matter, style, translations, mangalacharan—then verse-by-verse begins Wednesday.)

2) Central question driving “need to study”: Are you living or just existing?

He opens the “need” topic with a sharp diagnostic:

Living vs Existing

  • Living = moving forward through conscious choices, growth, direction
  • Existing = being stuck, postponing, drifting, not advancing

Why most people only “exist”

Because life forces choices, and choices provoke fear:

  • fear of being wrong
  • fear of consequences
  • fear of commitment

So the pattern becomes:

fear → hesitation → delay/postpone → no choice → no movement forward → “existing”

Time sensitivity he stresses

  • “With every sunset, one precious day is gone.”
  • So the issue isn’t philosophical only—it’s urgent.

3) His key life-skill: Decision-making + learning from mistakes

He doesn’t demand perfection in decisions. He demands movement and learning.

His practical rule

  • Make decisions.
  • Some will be wrong.
  • Learn.
  • Deepen understanding.
  • Improve future decisions.

Important caution: He warns against “life-ending” foolish decisions—he’s saying don’t freeze, but also don’t be reckless.

4) The engine of right living: Conviction

This is one of the strongest repeated themes.

What conviction does (as he repeats)

  • gives power
  • gives fuel/supply
  • gives direction

The “easiest rule” he gives

Make choices aligned with your conviction—never against it.

Why study Atmasiddhi then?

Because the text is meant to correct and strengthen convictions, especially about:

  • what is real vs changing
  • where happiness lies
  • who “I” am
  • what life is for

Without strong conviction, people:

  • become scared to live by their own understanding
  • copy others’ lives (“What do you think I should do?”)
  • seek constant external validation for decisions

He’s not condemning guidance—he says guidance from a Sadguru is helpful—but he criticizes the fear-based habit of outsourcing your life.

5) A very important nuance: Knowledge comes from outside, but conviction must become yours

He explains a key progression:

1. Knowledge may come from a person or books

2. But once the idea is absorbed and assimilated, it becomes yours

3. Then you can live by it with strength

His teaching method supports this

That’s why he refuses a “2-minute commandment style.”

  • If he just gave dos/don’ts, it would be quick.
  • But Param Krupalu Dev’s path is not blind compliance.
  • It must become your internal conviction.

Strong statement

  • No place for blind faith in true spirituality.

6) Why the commitment is so long: 145 hours = a “new birth” process

He uses a vivid metaphor:

Coming out of the “womb of ignorance”

  • Ignorance is the womb that produces endless desires (“fancies”)
  • Desires make us “beggars” and victims of situations

So spiritual study is like taking a new birth:

  • cannot be “premature delivery”
  • needs time to mature—he says “nine months” (and maybe a bit more)

Meaning:

  • Don’t rush transformation.
  • Let understanding grow into stable conviction.

7) From “admiring virtues” to becoming noble

He warns against a common spiritual trap:

  • just gazing at others’ virtues
  • appreciating saints/ideal people
  • stopping there

Instead:

  • develop at least a noble personality
  • if not divine/enlightened state, then at minimum:
    • mumukshuta (yearning for liberation)
    • qualities of an aspirant
    • a seeker’s inner maturity

This directly ties to “need to study”:

  • the text is a structured training to build an inner personality, not just information.

8) Dynamic life + inner calm (not laziness, not agitation)

He defines a desirable life as:

  • dynamic action externally (service, discipline, effort)
  • inner calm/equipoise internally

He strongly contrasts:

  • dynamic action (superior) vs
  • passive, slothful inaction (misery to self; burden to society)

He uses almost a civic framing:

  • idle life becomes a liability
  • life will “whip you” if you stay lazy

So “need to study” is partly:

  • to get out of drift,
  • to become purposeful, disciplined, useful.

9) The “inner equipment” model: why humans still aren’t perfected

This is a major conceptual block:

Outer equipment

  • human body, human form

Inner equipment

  • mind + intellect

He says:

  • physical evolution reaches a “complete” stage in human birth
  • but we neglect inner equipment
  • and it becomes clogged with:
    • false values
    • imaginary contentment (“all is well” mechanically)
    • compulsive desire fulfillment
    • indecision (“I’m not sure” even after 10 years)

Spirituality = re-tuning the inner equipment

  • Re-tuning mind and intellect under Sadguru’s guidance
  • Externally it may look “religious”
  • Internally it is true “spiritual life”
  • The world judges superficial dos/don’ts
  • The Sadguru judges motive, sincerity, perseverance

So Atmasiddhi becomes a manual for:

  • mental clarity
  • intellectual direction
  • value correction
  • inner purification

10) Discipline: “taming yourself” is non-negotiable

He becomes very direct here:

  • If you want progress, there is no place for:
    • moods
    • whims
    • passing fancies
  • Otherwise you become a slave of the mind
  • A slave struggles; cannot truly succeed (in the deeper sense)

He describes discipline as a “guaranteed path” to exit the mental cloud.

11) The desert/mirage teaching: Stop blaming the world for not giving happiness

One of the most memorable sections.

Key idea

Seeking happiness in the world is like looking for water in a desert:

  • desert is not rude—it simply doesn’t have water
  • the fault is the seeker looking in the wrong place

He supports it with a Gujarati proverb (he translates):

  • “Face in the mirror, happiness in the world—doesn’t exist, yet is seen.”

This connects tightly to the course’s aim:

  • Atmasiddhi corrects the distorted vision that makes us chase mirages.

12) Two illusions that create suffering

Illusion 1: treating the world as real & permanent

But the world is forever changing. So he suggests a “vow”:

  • never ask “why did it change?” Because change is the nature of the world.

If you insist on living outwardly:

  • be ready for uncertainty
  • don’t become insecure about it

Illusion 2: “more comfort = more happiness”

He calls this a persistent delusion—even after satsang. He suggests Atmasiddhi will change that conviction at root.

13) Prosperity without peace = calamity

He makes a blunt psychological-spiritual point:

  • material success without inner peace is not “winning”
  • it is a calamity

He adds another life observation:

  • even the greatest worldly joys carry a touch of sorrow:
    • fear of loss
    • insecurity
    • “what if” thoughts after celebrations
    • anxiety over possessions (example: fear of scratching a car)

So the need to study is not theoretical:

  • it’s a response to the built-in instability of worldly joy.

14) “Responsibilities” vs attachment (the sieve metaphor)

He says we label many things “responsibilities,” but often it’s attachment.

His metaphor:

  • put your “responsibilities” in a sieve
  • attachment stays on top
  • true responsibilities pass through

The underlying lesson:

  • the mind hides attachments behind respectable labels.
  • Atmasiddhi is meant to expose and purify this.

15) The missing enquiry: the 3 fundamentals of metaphysics

He says life passes without enquiry into:

1. World — what is it, what is its nature?

2. Soul (Self) — who am I, what is my nature?

3. God — what is God’s nature, grace vs self-effort?

He warns that without this enquiry, life becomes:

  • education → career → marriage → children → old age → death …all while calling it “responsibility.”

So the need to study is:

  • to awaken enquiry,
  • to stop sleepwalking through existence.

16) Human birth is “unasked” — therefore it has a definite purpose

He stresses:

  • you did not request human birth
  • it was given “unasked”
  • therefore it has a definite and sure purpose
  • the tragedy is wasting it in “a thousand ways”

He reframes “religion” as:

  • a challenging call to dynamic living
  • not merely a hobby

17) Foundation principle: false foundation → “castle of cards” life

One of the concluding ideas:

  • Life is built around your foundation (convictions, values, purpose)
  • If foundation is false:
    • life becomes a “castle of cards”
    • you can “have everything” but still feel emptiness

So the need to study Atmasiddhi is:

  • to rebuild foundation correctly,
  • and to “expand and experience inner grandeur.”

He ends by saying: for the “blessed ones,” this is not “higher purpose vs lower purpose.” This is simply the purpose.

19) Practical “application notes” (how to use this talk)

A) Daily self-check: living vs existing

Ask at night:

  • Did I make at least one clear decision today (instead of postponing)?
  • Was it aligned with my best conviction?

B) Conviction-building practice (simple)

Pick 1 area (e.g., happiness, identity, discipline) and write:

  • What do I currently believe?
  • Where did this belief come from (society, fear, past pain)?
  • What would a wiser conviction look like?

C) Re-tuning inner equipment

He implies a simple agenda:

  • reduce false values
  • strengthen discrimination
  • train mind away from fancy → toward purpose

D) Discipline “starter pack”

Choose 3 non-negotiables for 30 days:

1. fixed waking time

2. one daily study slot (even 20–30 min)

3. one restraint (food/speech/screen/etc.)
