Transition point: from “answers to doubts” → to “stepwise spiritual course”

The speaker says we’ve just completed a first major group:

  • Group 1: roughly verses 92–107 (or “Guru’s answers” 97–107) This group resolved the disciple’s doubts about the means/path of liberation:
    • undisputed path,
    • confusion due to many viewpoints,
    • caste/gender/attire discrimination.

Now begins:

  • Group 2: verses 108–113 These verses provide a step-by-step course of spiritual progress—like a compact “curriculum” of the path.

And later:

  • Group 3: 114–118 (he just names it here as the next block)

Key claim

Even though Jain spiritual development is classically described in 14 stages (gunasthānak), Param Krupalu Dev compresses the whole journey into six verses (108–113) to make it digestible.

He also maps key milestones:

  • Verse 108: the first step (eligibility / worthiness)
  • Verse 110: Self-realisation
  • Verse 113: Omniscience (kevaljnan)

So in this talk, we are at the very first step: “Who is fit to begin the journey?”

2) A theme he keeps repeating: “Every verse is complete in itself”

He strongly discourages the mindset of:

“This verse is incomplete; I need the next verse to understand it.”

His point:

  • Each verse on the path is complete and self-sufficient (it can be stated negatively/positively, but it stands on its own).
  • The “beauty” is that the whole path can be expressed in a few words, yet still be complete.

He uses a humorous everyday contrast:

  • Even explaining something simple (“what did you eat?” or “how to make beans on toast”) can’t be done fully in 10 words…
  • Yet the path of liberation is captured in just 8–9 words per verse.

Takeaway: Don’t wait for “perfect completeness.” Catch what strikes your heart in a verse and start practicing it.

3) Verse 108: what it’s doing (core purpose)

Verse 108 is an “eligibility verse”

It defines: Who is a real aspirant—worthy to begin the path of supreme truth.

He summarizes verse 108 into four virtues. If they are present (or being cultivated), the person is called:

  • Jignāsu = a truth seeker (aspirant)

He also links it to verse 38, saying verse 108 repeats the same four core traits with almost no change—because Param Krupalu Dev values clarity over variety.

4) The four eligibility-virtues in verse 108

The speaker lists them clearly:

1. Kashayni upshantata

2. Matra moksh-abhilash

3. Bhavey khed

4. Antar daya …and says: one who has these is called jignasu.

Let’s break them down the way the speaker does.

4.1 Kashayni Upshantata

Meaning: passions have “subsided” (calmed down)

Key nuance he stresses: At this beginner stage, you are not expected to destroy passions completely.

  • Not “total conquest”
  • Not “perfection”
  • But pacifying / weakening / calming down

He emphasizes it should happen:

  • with understanding
  • with firmness
  • not as a mood-cycle (“angry today, sorry tomorrow”)

Memory hook he uses: Pacific Ocean

He plays with the word “Pacific”:

  • pacific = peaceful
  • pacifying = making peaceful He even references “Prashant Samudra / Mahasagar” as the “peaceful ocean” analogy.

Practical meaning: you are training the inner climate so anger/ego/etc become milder, less frequent, less intense.

4.2 Matra Moksh Abhilash

Meaning: exclusive / burning desire for liberation

He makes this very sharp:

  • “Desire for liberation” is common (people even want “liberation from spouse / mother-in-law,” etc.)
  • But here it means exclusive desire for the true liberation.

He insists you must use an adjective, like:

  • exclusive
  • obsessive (he says this is one of the rare times he uses “obsession” positively)
  • burning passion

The energy principle (big teaching point)

He says you can’t destroy energy—you redirect it.

So:

  • Don’t try to “remove passion-energy”
  • Redirect it:
    • away from anger/ego/deceit/greed
    • toward liberation

He even states it as a kind of spiritual strategy:

“Subsidence of passions by increasing the passion for liberation.”

That’s the engine: one passion replaces another, but in the right direction.

4.3 Bhavey Khed

Meaning: grief / weariness about embodiment & transmigration

(and about your own impure inner cycles)

He defines it as being tired of:

  • the wheel of birth/death/transmigration, and also
  • the inner wheel:
    • desire erupts → mistake → regret → vow → repeats again

So bhavey khed is not just “sadness.” It’s a mature spiritual exhaustion:

  • “I’ve seen the futility.”
  • “I still can’t stop.”
  • “Enough is enough; I want out of this loop.”

Connection to Jay Viyaray Sutra (important detail in this talk)

He notes that the same cluster of meanings appears in the Jay Viyaray prayer, especially in phrases like:

  • disaffection for transmigration,
  • following the path,
  • attaining the desirable fruit,
  • abandoning what is socially/spiritually degrading.

His point: the tradition’s daily prayers are pointing to the same core inner orientation.

The “love test” (one of the most practical parts)

He gives a very usable psychological test:

  • If you do something and feel “it was too little” → you are in love with it
  • If you feel “it’s too much / a burden / formality” → it’s mechanical ritual

He gives relatable examples:

  • 2 hours with someone you love still feels “less”
  • 2 minutes with someone you don’t love feels “enough”

He applies it to spiritual practices:

  • bhakti / prayers / seva should start becoming something you feel is “never enough,” not “a duty I must finish.”

4.4 Antar Daya

Meaning: inner compassion (compassion from within)

He contrasts:

  • Pranidaya (verse 38) = compassion for all living beings
  • Antar daya (verse 108) = compassion from within

He says they are not contradictory—same essence—but antar daya adds depth:

  • not compassion because “I’m a Jain so I should…”
  • but compassion that arises naturally inside

Two layers of antar daya (very important)

He offers two ways to understand it:

1. Compassion for oneself

  • a deep pity/sympathy for your own soul:
  • “So many lives of pain, yet I still keep the causes of bondage.”
  • He uses a powerful traditional image:
    • if you gathered the bones from all your births they’d exceed the Himalayas,
    • tears shed would exceed the oceans.
  • Meaning: you feel an urgent inner mercy: “Enough. I must free myself from impurities.”

2. Compassion for all

  • He says: when a pure-hearted person develops compassion for self in the spiritual sense,
  • it naturally extends outward into compassion for others.

So antar daya becomes:

  • inner tenderness + inner sincerity + genuine goodwill toward beings.

5) What verse 108 is not saying

He explicitly warns against skipping prerequisites.

He uses a PhD eligibility story:

  • If you haven’t even done 12th / BA / MA etc., you can’t demand a PhD structure.
  • Likewise:
    • don’t jump to “omniscience talk” as your immediate concern,
    • first build worthiness (eligibility).
His framing:
  • These four virtues do not make you enlightened.
  • They make you eligible to begin the path properly.

6) How to recognize a true seeker (very “testable”)

He challenges the common mistake:

Is a true seeker recognized by:

  • clothes?
  • hours of silence?
  • number of chants?
  • external ritual intensity?

He says: No. A true seeker is recognized by the four inner virtues.

He gives a living example (Prashant) to show:

  • virtues can’t remain hidden,
  • the teacher naturally “feels drawn” to such a person,
  • and he specifically points out seeing:
    • subsided passions,
    • desire for liberation,
    • grief for embodiment,
    • inner compassion.

7) Why Param Krupalu Dev repeats the same 4 virtues from verse 38

The speaker makes a devotional + instructional point:

  • Most authors would change words to give “variety.”
  • Param Krupalu Dev repeats because he wants:
    • clarity
    • purity
    • not display of vocabulary

So if verse 38 and verse 108 look similar, that’s not lack of words—it’s deliberate insistence on the essentials.

8) Small terminology note: “Atmarthi” vs “Jignasu”

He says:

  • Atmarthi (verse 38) and Jignasu (verse 108) mean almost the same practically:
    • true seeker / aspirant of liberation

But he hints:

  • “Why the term jignasu here?” becomes clearer in verse 109 (likely because the next step involves guidance from a true Guru).

9) Practical takeaways: how to start “living verse 108”

Here are grounded ways to practice each virtue (aligned with the transcript’s intent):

A) Kashayni Upshantata (pacify passions)

  • Track one dominant passion (anger / ego / etc.) for 7 days.
  • Don’t aim for “never again.”
  • Aim for:
    • reduced intensity,
    • shorter duration,
    • quicker recovery,
    • fewer justifications.

Daily question: “Was it milder today than last week?”

B) Matra Moksh Abhilash (exclusive burning desire)

  • Write one line daily:
    • “My final goal is liberation / pure state.”
  • Before entertainment/impulse choices, ask:
    • “Does this strengthen my direction or dilute it?”

Daily question: “Where did my energy go today—bondage direction or liberation direction?”

C) Bhavey Khed (weariness of the cycle)

  • Do honest journaling:
    • “What pattern repeats in me?”
    • “What regret loop do I keep living?”
  • Let the weariness become fuel, not self-hate.

Daily question: “Am I sincerely tired of this pattern—or secretly still in love with it?”

D) Antar Daya (inner compassion)

  • Practice compassion in two directions:

1. toward your own soul: “Enough suffering—let me purify.”

2. toward beings: reduce harshness, contempt, cruelty in speech/thought.

Daily question: “When something wrong happened, did compassion arise—or ego/anger?”

10) Quick revision tools

A) One-line essence of verse 108

Eligibility begins when passions calm down, liberation becomes the exclusive thirst, the soul feels weary of wandering, and compassion arises from within.

B) 4-word memory chain (easy)

Calm → Crave → Tire → Care
  • Calm passions (upshantata)
  • Crave liberation only (moksh-abhilash)
  • Tire of transmigration (bhavey khed)
  • Care from within (antar daya)
