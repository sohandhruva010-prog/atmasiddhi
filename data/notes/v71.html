• The speaker frames this as the disciple’s doubts arising under the influence of “well-known philosophies/schools of thought.”
  • The focus is the Third Fundamental Truth:
    • Doership of the soul (karta-bhav / “karta” = doer).
  • Specifically, in the Mokshmala verse sequence, they are entering verse 71, where:
    • The disciple argues against the soul being the doer of karma.
    • The Guru will later argue that the soul is the doer (and answer these doubts across later verses).

2) Core statement of Verse 71 (what the disciple says)

The disciple’s main claim

  • “Karta jiva na karmano” = The soul is NOT the doer of karma.
    • Speaker repeats and drills the meaning:
      • karta = doer
      • jiva = soul
      • karmano = of karma

Why this matters (in the disciple’s mind)

  • The disciple is already convinced of:
    • the existence of the soul
    • the existence of karma (as the cause behind observed diversities)
  • Now the refined question is:
    • If karma is the cause of diversity, then who is the doer/creator of karma?
  • The disciple’s stance in verse 71:
    • “Not the soul.”
    • Therefore: “Then who?”

3) The disciple offers 3 options (Verse 71 gives three alternatives)

The speaker says: in this verse the disciple gives three options. (He’ll give two more options in the next verse, and then he’ll conclude again that the soul isn’t the doer.)

Option 1: “Karma j karta karma” — karmas do karmas

  • Meaning:
    • karma itself is the doer of karma
    • present karmas arise from past karmas, and so on, endlessly
  • In simple chain language:
    • past → present → future (karma generating karma)

Option 2: “Athva sahaj swabhav” — karmas bind automatically, without specific reason

  • Meaning:
    • karmas arise/bind spontaneously, accidentally, “just happens”
    • no specific cause-condition reasoning required
  • This mirrors a worldview that rejects strict cause-effect inquiry at a deeper level.

Option 3: “Ka (or) karma jivno dharma” — karma is the soul’s nature

  • Meaning:
    • doing karmas / acquiring karmas is the dharma (nature/property) of the soul
    • if something is “nature,” you don’t ask why—like “why is bliss the nature, not sorrow?”
  • Implication:
    • if karma is the soul’s inherent nature, the soul will never stop doing/acquiring karmas.

4) Speaker’s teaching style here: he makes the “options” vivid with humor and relatable examples

This part isn’t random—it’s how he makes abstract philosophy “stick.”

“Choose an option” humor (wives/food choices)

  • He jokes that the disciple is giving the Guru “options,” like:
    • some people ask open-ended “what do you want to eat?” (creates more work)
    • some offer constrained choices (idli/dosa, cuisines etc.)
  • Teaching purpose:
    • makes the disciple seem thoughtful and structured
    • shows the disciple has already pre-processed the problem into alternatives

Genetics example (XX/XY) used to illustrate “mechanism vs deeper why”

  • He restates: XX = girl, XY = boy
  • The disciple’s pressure point:
    • even if you explain the genetics, at some point you hit:
      • “why this outcome this time?”
    • and many people end up saying: “automatic.”

Friend story: “why so many boys?” (Jitendra Punjabi / brothers)

  • He tells a real school story:
    • friend had multiple brothers
    • friend says mother wanted a girl “so tried again and again”
    • then the friend flips it back: “ask your mother why she went for the third”
  • Teaching purpose:
    • shows how “why” questions can become personal/ego-touching
    • shows the “why” chain never ends cleanly through surface explanations

Medical example (fit person dies / “it just happened”)

  • He references someone asking:
    • “Why did a healthy person’s heart stop?”
  • He says eventually medicine may stop at:
    • “It happened.”
  • Teaching purpose:
    • illustrates Option 2’s mindset: don’t over-commit to cause-effect explanations

“Every effect has a cause” challenge (quiz + surname joke)

  • He recalls someone saying: “every effect has to have a cause”
  • He humorously challenges it:
    • “Why? because your surname is Sarkar (government) so it must be true?”
  • Teaching purpose:
    • introduces a worldview where “uncaused events” (or events without knowable reasons) are allowed

5) A crucial conceptual detour: “karma” has two meanings (and he will use both)

This is one of the most important parts of the lecture because it prevents confusion later.

Meaning 1: Karma = action (common usage)

  • In most traditions (and generally), karma means action.
  • He uses the Bhagavad Gita structure as a quick reference:
    • first six chapters: Karma Yoga (action + union with Divine)
    • next six: Bhakti Yoga (devotion)
    • last six: Jnana Yoga (knowledge)
  • Point: in that context, karma ≠ particles; karma = actions.

Meaning 2: Karma (Jain sense) = karmic material particles that bind the soul

  • In Jainism, karma is taught as:
    • subtle material particles that cling to the soul
    • form layers
    • later manifest as “fruits” (inner/outer results)
  • He notes:
    • outside Jainism, many accept karma as a law (“you sow, you reap”),
    • but not as literal particles clinging to soul.

How he will disambiguate terms in his teaching

  • When he means action, he may say “doing karmas.”
  • When he means particle bondage, he may say “acquiring karmas.”
  • He gives a practical example:
    • listening right now = “auspicious karma” (action)
    • later drinking Red Bull to relax = “inauspicious karma” (action)
    • both are “karma” as action

Flour analogy (to explain subtle karmic matter)

  • He compares:
    • wheat flour vs refined flour (maida)
    • same substance, but one is finer
    • finer particles clump/stick more (he jokes about it sticking in the “gut”)
  • Moral of analogy:
    • karmic matter is extremely subtle (still “pudgal” = matter), hence not visible
    • but can “stick” due to subtlety

Side note: “You are my karma, you are my dharma”

  • He warns: in devotional language (Vedantic usage), “karma” may mean:
    • “all my actions are for you,” not “I want karmic bondage”
  • Teaching purpose:
    • don’t let a Jain meaning override a non-Jain usage in poetry/devotion

6) Detailed unpacking of the 3 options (what each implies and why the disciple likes it)

The speaker keeps returning to the same lens:

  • the disciple wants to preserve the soul’s purity (untouched/unbound),
  • so he is reluctant to make the soul responsible for karma.

Option 1 unpacked: “karma causes karma” (chain model)

He explains it in BOTH meanings:

A) If karma = actions
  • One action triggers reactions.
  • Reaction itself becomes another action → chain continues.
  • Result:
    • you get locked into an endless chain: past → present → future
B) If karma = karmic particles (bondage)
  • existing karmas “prompt” new karmic particles to be attracted to the soul
  • the “drama” is between old and new karmas
  • soul is merely:
    • witness
    • untouched, unbound
  • He even says:
    • “This is going to be your sadhana (practice) — be a witness.”
    • Important nuance: he calls it a practice instruction, not the final truth.
Problem implied by Option 1 (as the disciple sees it):
  • If karmas alone bind karmas, the soul becomes helpless:
    • you can’t even avoid influx
    • bondage happens regardless
  • This supports the disciple’s claim:
    • “soul is not the doer”
Preview of the Guru’s refutation (he explicitly points to verse 74)
  • He quotes the gist of the rebuttal:
    • If there is no inspiration from consciousness (chetan), who ‘grasps’ karma?
    • Lifeless matter has no capacity to inspire (jadswabhav nahi prerna).
  • He uses a physical-object image:
    • matter doesn’t “feel like” jumping up and hitting someone’s forehead
    • only a conscious being “feels” and initiates throwing
  • Underlying principle the Guru will use:
    • action/binding needs a prompter
    • matter alone doesn’t self-initiate purposive binding

Option 2 unpacked: “sahaj swabhav” (automatic/no specific reason)

  • He calls this attractive and says many “intelligent people” answer this way:
    • “it just happened”
  • He applies it to:
    • medicine (“why did he die? it happened”)
    • COVID (“sick person didn’t get it; healthy did—why? it happened”)
    • genetics outcomes (XX/XY without deeper reason)
  • Core idea:
    • karmas (actions or particles) unfold automatically,
    • without soul’s effort and without a specific reason-condition chain
Implication for liberation/responsibility:
  • If it’s automatic and not under the soul’s agency,
    • the soul isn’t responsible
    • and it becomes hard to justify liberation through effort

Option 3 unpacked: “karma is the soul’s dharma (nature)”

  • Claim:
    • the soul cannot be without activity
    • so it will always be doing something (mind/speech/body)
    • you only “choose” whether it’s good or bad activity
Audience interaction (Maulik + correction)
  • Someone argues:
    • if activity were inherent, all souls would always be active
    • but there are souls without activity: Siddhas (liberated souls)
  • Speaker clarifies a key distinction:
    • embodied souls (with mind/speech/body) are continuously in activity
    • bodiless karmaless state has no activity (in the “action” sense)
Major philosophical implication he highlights
  • If karma (bondage/activity) is the soul’s essential nature:
    • you can never stop it
    • therefore liberation is impossible
  • He states it in terms of the “Fundamental Truths”:
    • endorsing this option would eliminate the Fifth Fundamental Truth (“There is Liberation”).

7) The disciple’s conclusion at the end of this verse (what he’s trying to establish)

The speaker summarizes the disciple’s intent:

  • In all three options, the outcome is:
    • the soul is not responsible for doing/acquiring karma
  • Also, each option creates serious consequences for the path:
    • Option 1 → helpless bondage (karmas bind karmas)
    • Option 2 → randomness/automaticity undermines agency
    • Option 3 → inherent nature undermines liberation itself
  • Therefore, the disciple is setting up a firm stance:
    • “Soul is not the doer of karmas.”

He ends with a preview:

  • “Other two philosophies we will be taking tomorrow.”
    • i.e., two more options will be presented later (matching the earlier “five options” structure).
  • “Let’s sing this verse.” (signals transition from explanation to recitation/practice)
