2. Transition to the New Section: “Matārthinā Lakṣaṇo” (Attributes of a Bigot)

The speaker explains the move into the next section, in which the text addresses a particular category: those who are engaged in religious/spiritual activity, but with fundamental misunderstandings. Key observations:

  • He emphasises that the soul (ātman) has, since beginningless time, been undergoing transmigration (in gatis: human, animal/plant, hellish beings, celestial) because of intense delusion (moha).
  • The soul has experienced infinite misery (paṃyo duḥkha ananta) but remained ignorant in the dense darkness of these gatis.
  • Importantly: “Not that he never got an opportunity of the right religion” — i.e., even with opportunities (via his punya, past karmic merit), due to negative impressions and false beliefs about religion, he fails to understand or realise the importance of true religion.
  • So these folks may be reading scriptures, performing rituals, etc., but because they are stuck (either in external ritualism or in barren knowledge) they are not true seekers.

Key takeaway: It’s not merely engaging in religious activity that defines a true seeker — but the understanding, the intent, the inner alignment and truth-understanding. If one is stuck in superficial forms, one remains in bondage.

3. Difference & Similarity: The Two “Bigot” Types vs. a Worldly Person

The speaker draws an important contrast and similarity:

  • Difference: The two types (mechanical ritualist and dry intellectualist) differ from the worldly person because they do something religious: one performs rituals/studies scriptures; the worldly person doesn’t even bother.
  • Similarity: Despite the difference in activity, the similarity is that neither knows what true religion is (i.e., the purpose, the soul, liberation). The mechanical ritualist is stuck in external form; the dry intellectualist is stuck in knowledge without transformation.
  • Because of improper understanding of religion/spirituality, both cannot end transmigration.

Example/Reflection: Imagine someone who attends all religious ceremonies, does many fasts, visits places of worship — but does so purely externally (for show, social prestige, habit). They may seem righteous yet inwardly lack the core insight: who am I? What is the purpose of this? They may never experience the freedom and awakening that true religion offers.

4. Purpose of This Section: Why “Attributes of a Bigot”?

The speaker clarifies the intention behind this section (verses 24–33):

  • It is not to condemn or shame people — i.e., not to degrade.
  • Rather it’s to identify (reflect) → remove (eliminate) traits of the bigot, so one can progress into being a true seeker. (“R – R”: Reflect, Remove)
  • If one sees a trait within oneself, one can put in effort toward the path of liberation.

This is important: the teaching isn’t just theoretical, but practical and introspective.

5. Breakdown of Verses 24–33 (This Section)

The speaker gives an organised layout of this section:

  • Verses 24–33 → a total of 10 stanzas
  • These 10 are divided into two sub-categories:

1. Verses 24–28 (5 stanzas): Attributes of the kriyajāda matārthi (mechanical ritualist-bigot)

  • Verse 24: False beliefs regarding the Guru
  • Verse 25: False beliefs regarding the Omniscient Lord
  • Verse 26: (implied) Another aspect of belief involving the Lord/Guru etc
  • Verse 27: False beliefs about scriptures / external dress / form
  • Verse 28: False beliefs about austerities/vows/penance

2. Verses 29–31 (3 stanzas): Attributes of the śuṣk-jñānī matārthi (dry-intellectualist-bigot)

  • One single thematic aspect across 3 verses

3. Verse 32: Common attributes of both bigots (mechanical ritualist and dry intellectualist) — four traits: passions not subsided, no detachment, no straightforwardness, no impartial vision.

4. Verse 33: Conclusion of this section and introduction to the next section (attributes of a true seeker).

The speaker emphasises: Why did the text need 10 verses for the “bigot” rather than just one? Because the pitfalls are many, and we must be clear which traits to watch for.

6. Illustrative Example / Poetic Analogy

The speaker uses a poem by Harivansh Rai Bachchan (“Madhushala”) to illustrate how sectarian vision causes division:

“Vair karave mandir-masjid; mel karave madhushala.” Temples and mosques provoke enmity; a pub creates unity.

The point being: If we don’t understand what true religion is, if we cling to sectarian labels (“Hindu-Jain”, “Śvetāmbara-Digambara”, etc.), then even what we call love for mission / religion may actually be ego or fanaticism, not real spiritual insight.

Reflection for you (Khushi): In your self-care/spiritual growth context, this is a wake-up call — to check whether your spiritual practice is inclusive, humble, centred on the soul, rather than comparative or ego-driven.

7. Core Traits of the Bigot (Matārthi) — Negative Signs

Here are some of the specific traits the speaker highlights (especially in verse 32) that you should reflect on:

  • Passions (kaṣāya) have not subsided — When someone does many religious activities but inside remains ruled by anger, pride, envy, attachment, etc.
  • There is no inner detachment (vairāgya) — All is done for show, for getting praise, for fulfilling some external standard, rather than inner freedom.
  • No straightforwardness (satyata / frankness) — One may hide one’s faults, not confess; or one may be rigid, inflexible, always defending one’s view.
  • No impartial vision (sama-darśana / equanimity) — Someone may favour their sect, their group, their mode, and oppose others; their vision is partial, not universal.

The speaker emphasises: You can perform many sevas, study many scriptures, but if these qualities are absent — you’re still a bigot, not a true seeker (atmārthi).

8. Encouragement to Self-Assess

The speaker asks the audience to be honest: “Are you engaged in many religious activities? Study, service, etc. But still: Are you a true seeker or just a bigot?” The message to you (Khushi) is: Use this section as introspection:

  • Identify which of the traits listed (verses 24–33) you may have.
  • Reflect: “Is my devotion genuine, or is it driven by self-will, ego, sectarianism?”
  • Remove: Actively work on cultivating detachment, equanimity, inner stillness.

9. Preview of the Next Section: Attributes of a True Seeker (Atmārthinā Lakṣaṇo)

Finally, the speaker sets forward the next stage:

  • After describing the negative (what not to have / what traps to avoid), the text will go into the positive: What is a true seeker?
  • Verses 34–42 cover the attributes of an atmārthi (true seeker).
  • The approach will be dual: negative (describe pitfalls) and positive (describe what to cultivate).
  • The benefit: If the teaching were only negative (just “don’t do this”), it wouldn’t inspire growth. But since the next section will show positive qualities, aspirants are motivated: “Let me become this.”

10. Summary of Key Meaning – Why This Matters

For your self-care/spiritual journey (Khushi), the core messages to take away:

  • It’s not sufficient to do spiritual/religious work; the inner orientation, the understanding, the purity of motive, the alignment with truth matter more.
  • The “bigot” (matārthi) is someone who looks spiritual but lacks inner transformation. The lesson is to beware of self-deception: outward form without inner freedom is still bondage.
  • True religion/spirituality is rooted in right perception (samyaktva), humility, self-effort (purushartha), and inner stillness.
  • The journey invites continuous reflection: “Which traits of the bigot do I see in me? How do I remove them?”
  • Then moving into cultivating the positive: detachment, equanimity, inner witness consciousness, etc.
  • Ultimately the goal is liberation — not just religious practice, but real freedom of the soul.
