He starts with the key line:

“To accept the existence of soul is the first step on the path of liberation.”
  • Why are we doing any of this?
    • Because we want liberation.
  • Liberation of whom?
    • Of the soul.
  • So:

If you’re not even convinced the soul exists, there is no starting point for mokshmarg.

Therefore:

  • It is crucial to be completely doubtless about:

The existence of the soul.

Param Krupalu Dev devotes:

  • 14 verses (45–58) to just this first fundamental:
    • 4 verses → disciple’s doubts (45–48),
    • 10 verses → Sadguru’s answers (49–58).

He recaps structure:

  • Intro: 23
  • Matārthī: 10
  • Ātmārthī: 9
  • Shatpad-Nāmkathan (intro to 6 fundamentals): 2
  • Now: 1st Fundamental – 14 verses.

2. Charvaka influence & his MA professor story

Verses 45–48 present:

The disciple’s doubt about the existence of soul, which has arisen under influence of Charvaka-like materialism.

He had written “atheistic Charvaka” in the notes but now corrects:

  • Technically, “atheistic” means:
    • Not believing in God.
  • Our topic here is:
    • Existence of soul, not God.
  • Jainism also doesn’t accept a creator-God, so many call it “atheistic” too.
  • So he says:

“Let’s drop ‘atheistic’ here. Just say: Charvaka – pure materialism.”

Then he shares a personal story:

  • During his MA in Philosophy, he went 4 days a week to Kalina University.
  • His Dean, Professor Antarkar:
    • Highly learned,
    • A Charvaka – didn’t believe in:
      • Soul,
      • God,
      • Karma.
    • In other words:

“Nothing of the chha pada.”

Yet:

  • He was one of the most wonderful people he has met.
  • He taught Western philosophy brilliantly:
    • Logical atomism & logical positivism (UK),
    • Pragmatism (USA),
    • Existentialism (Europe, especially France).

Between lectures:

  • They would sit in his cabin and “fight”:
    • As if:
      • He wanted the professor to become a Jain,
      • Professor wanted him to become Charvaka.
  • After 20–25 years, the professor came to his satsang at Yogi Sabhagruh:
    • He fell at the professor’s feet:

“You taught me to question and reason. That logical rigour is your obligation on me.”

So:

  • He has a living, affectionate respect for a Charvaka teacher,
  • Even while disagreeing with his denial of soul.

This is the mindset in which he now presents the disciple’s Charvaka-style doubt.

3. Verse 45 – the disciple’s first argument

Param Krupalu Dev, in the disciple’s role, says:

Nathi drashtimā āvto, nathi jānātu rūp;
Bījo pan anubhav nahi, tethi na jīvswaroop.

He unpacks:

1. “Nathi drashtimā āvto” – The soul cannot be seen.

  • I can see:
    • Book, clock, people, trees.
  • But I never see “soul”.

2. “Nathi jānātu rūp” – Its form is unknown:

  • Nobody can say:
    • “It’s triangular,”
    • “Square,”
    • “Round,”
    • “Colourless,” etc.
  • Even Enlightened Ones don’t specify a form or colour.
  • So:

“We have no idea what ‘soul’ even looks like.”

3. “Bījo pan anubhav nahi” – It cannot be experienced by any other senses:

  • Air:
    • Invisible, but felt by touch.
  • Sound:
    • Not seen, but heard.
  • Smell, taste, touch:
    • Other unseen things can be grasped.
  • But soul:

“I can’t taste, smell, hear, or touch it.”

4. “Tethi na jīvswaroop” – Therefore:

“Soul does not exist.”

He summarises the disciple’s logic:

  • Soul:
    • Cannot be seen,
    • Has no known form,
    • Cannot be experienced by any senses.
  • Therefore:

“It is not reasonable to believe in its existence. People just talk about soul; they have never seen, known, or experienced it. So I conclude: no such thing as soul exists.”

He cautions:

“‘Therefore’ (tethi na jīvswaroop) here is not Sadguru’s conclusion. It is the disciple’s conclusion, voiced by Shrimad in the disciple’s role.”

He calls this Shrimad’s “triple role”:

  • When he doubts → He is disciple.
  • When he answers → He is Sadguru.
  • Sometimes → He is just Truth.

4. Hidden subtlety: “seen–known–experienced” = darshan–jnān–charitra

He reflects on the three phrases:

1. Cannot be seen,

2. Cannot be known,

3. Cannot be experienced.

He then links:

  • See = darshan,
  • Know = jnān,
  • Experience in conduct = charitra.

These three – darshan, jnān, charitra – are:

The very main attributes of the soul, and also the three jewels of the path.

He hints:

  • Disciple is unknowingly rejecting:
    • The very attributes that define spiritual progress:
      • Right seeing (darshan),
      • Right knowing (jnān),
      • Right experiencing/acting (charitra).
  • Sadguru will gently turn this around.

He says:

“If this feels confusing, you may ignore it for now;
just understand the surface logic: soul cannot be seen, known, experienced → so disciple denies it.”

5. Questioning as the beginning of the quest

He emphasises:

“The quest begins with questions.”

He loves a thinking, questioning mind:

  • If someone has no questions:

“I feel: nothing significant is going to happen here.”

But:

  • Too many questions can be a nuisance too. 😄

He shares a playful story:

  • In Denver, Krish bombarded Nemiji with questions:
    • “Do this today,”
    • Krish: 4–5 follow-up questions to do it perfectly.
  • Nemiji, frustrated, mentally drew a question mark on his forehead.
  • Moral:
    • Too few questions = sleeping mind,
    • Too many = restless mind,
    • Some sincere questions = healthy quest.

6. Different “purviews” of senses and logic

He says:

“A thinking person questions whatever doesn’t fall in the purview of his senses and his logic.”

He stresses “his”:

  • Everyone’s:
    • Senses and logical range differ.

Example:

  • You say:

“Don’t you have any common sense?”

  • But:
    • What you call “common” is just your sense:
      • Maybe your logical range is wider than theirs.
      • That doesn’t mean they are stupid; they just operate in a different “bandwidth.”

Similarly:

  • This disciple:
    • Has a limited purview:
      • If soul doesn’t appear in that,
      • He questions its existence.

He also uses an Ashram example:

  • People worry:
    • “How will 50,000 people be managed on these small roads?”
  • They don’t know:
    • Traffic & signage consultants have been engaged,
    • Multiple entry/exit strategies exist.
  • People assume:

“If I can’t see the plan, there is no plan.”

He uses this analogy to show:

“Just because something is outside your current logic & senses, does not mean it doesn’t exist.”

7. Whole life trained to trust only senses: the empirical trap

He explains:

  • From childhood, we’re trained:
    • “See properly,”
    • “Listen carefully,”
    • “Memorise what you see & hear.”

This builds:

The authority of senses.

Thus:

  • Anything beyond senses feels:
    • Ridiculous,
    • False,
    • Impossible,
    • Illusory.

He introduces the word empirical:

  • Empirical = what can be:
    • Perceived by senses, or
    • Grasped by logical inference.
  • UK philosophy:
    • Logical atomism, logical positivism:
      • He studied a whole year of these.
  • Those schools say:

“Only what can be empirically verified is meaningful / worth believing.”

So:

“If you can show me the soul, I’ll believe. If not, it’s rubbish.”

There are two types of people stuck here:

1. Those who believe:

  • Nothing exists beyond senses + logic.

2. Those who might admit something exists beyond, but:

  • Are so materially satisfied that:
    • “Why bother? I’m happy with parties, shopping, gossip, etc.”

He tells a “Madhur Milan” story:

  • His mother once belonged to a ladies’ group:
    • Madhur Milan – 40 women,
    • Meeting at each other’s house to gossip, play games, sing light songs.
  • One such meeting at his house when he was young:
    • He watched “butterflies” (his word) come and chat for hours.
    • Afterward, he told his mother:

“Take a vow and resign from this group today if you want to walk the spiritual path.”

  • Later, she moved into a purely spiritual circle.

Point:

Entire lives can be spent at the level of senses & social noise, losing out on deeper, richer inner experiences beyond senses.

8. Disciple’s state: materialist but sincere, not lazy

He clarifies:

  • This disciple is not:
    • Against white clothes,
    • Against temples,
    • Or against rituals per se.
  • He’s not rejecting soul out of:
    • Hatred,
    • Ego against religious people.

He is:

A thinking person whose world so far is empirical. He cannot see the soul, doesn’t see any sign, so he says, “I honestly don’t see why I should believe in it.”

This is different from:

  • Someone who says:
    • “I don’t feel like believing in soul. I want enjoyment.”
  • Here, it’s:

“My senses and logic don’t catch it – this is my problem.”

Sadguru sees:

“Because he is thinking, there’s a way to enter his mind.”

9. Summarising the disciple’s argument (Verse 45)

The disciple’s first argument boils down to:

1. “I can’t see the soul.”

  • My eyes can see many things clearly:
    • Books, clocks, bodies, etc.
  • So eyes are working.
  • If soul existed, why can’t these functioning eyes see it?

2. “Its form is unknown.”

  • Even Enlightened Ones don’t give a concrete description:
    • No clear shape, colour, outline.
  • So we have nothing identifiable to point at.

3. “It cannot be experienced by other senses either.”

  • Many invisible things can be sensed otherwise:
    • Air – felt by touch,
    • Sound – heard,
    • Smell – nose,
    • Taste – tongue.
  • Soul:
    • none of these detect it.

4. “Therefore, it does not exist.”

  • Without any perceptible sign,
  • Without any sensory basis,
  • Without any empirical evidence,
  • He concludes:

“There is no reason to believe in a distinct, unique substance called ‘soul’. Talking about it is just empty words.”

He closes this talk emphasising:

This is the honest starting point for many modern minds: “If I can’t see, know, or sense it – I doubt its existence.” From here, Sadguru will begin His patient dismantling in the coming verses.
