Absolutely. This satsang picks up exactly where the previous one ended. Gurudev has finished explaining the Six Fundamental Truths, the disciple has attained apurva bhan / Self-realisation, and now something naturally erupts within him: overwhelming gratitude toward his Sadguru.

The central verse is:

“Aho! Aho! Shri Sadguru, Karunasindhu Apar;
Aa pamar par Prabhu karyo, Aho! Aho! Upkar.”

In essence:

“O! O! My Enlightened Guru, You are a boundless ocean of compassion. On this worthless/unworthy being, O Lord, You have bestowed such immeasurable benevolence. Aho! Aho!”

And Gurudev's emphasis throughout the satsang is that this verse shouldn't merely be understood—it has to be felt.

Atmasiddhi — “Aho! Aho! Shri Sadguru”

1. What has happened to the disciple?

Param Krupalu Dev is presenting the Six Fundamental Truths as though the disciple is narrating his own experience.

Now, in verses 124–127, the disciple turns toward his Guru.

He has just made an extraordinary discovery:

He has discovered his own Self.

He is submerged in the bliss and excitement of Self-realisation. But alongside that joy, another emotion automatically arises:

Gratitude.

And importantly, it is gratitude accompanied by complete humility.

This connects beautifully to the previous satsang:

Self-realisation → recognition of what the Guru has done → gratitude.

The deeper the disciple understands what he has received, the deeper his gratitude becomes.

2. Why does he only say “Aho! Aho!”?

This is one of Gurudev's main points.

The disciple wants to thank his Guru.

But every word he considers feels inadequate.

“Immense?”

Too small.

“Greatest?”

Still too small.

“Boundless?”

Even that doesn't capture it.

The more he remembers what his Guru has done for him, the less capable he feels of describing it.

So eventually…

Words fail.

And all that comes out is:

“Aho! Aho!”

It isn't really a description.

It's an exclamation arising from experience.

3. Gurudev's “Wow!” example

Gurudev gives a very relatable explanation.

When you encounter something extraordinary, sometimes you don't immediately say:

“This possesses such-and-such characteristics…”

You simply say:

“WOW.”

Maybe you see something incredibly beautiful.

Maybe you taste an amazing drink at the café.

For a moment you're deciding:

  • tasty?
  • delicious?
  • marvelous?
  • extraordinary?

But the experience is greater than your vocabulary.

So:

“Wow!”

comes out.

Gurudev says “Aho!” functions similarly here.

The disciple is essentially overwhelmed:

“Wow… my Guru.”

Not casual amazement, of course, but an exclamation filled with:

reverence + awe + devotion + gratitude + love.

4. What does the disciple actually want now?

After attaining Self-realisation, what does he ask his Guru for?

Not worldly things.

Not achievements.

Not recognition.

Gurudev describes his feeling as:

“I want nothing. Just Your blessings and Your compassion.”

He wants to remember and worship his Guru constantly—almost as though the Guru were his very soul.

There is nothing higher for him anymore.

And Gurudev makes an important point here:

Someone who thinks,

“I achieved all this myself.”

cannot experience this gratitude.

If ego takes credit:

“My intelligence.” “My effort.” “My good karma.” “My ability.”

then Guru-upkar cannot truly be felt.

Gratitude becomes possible when the disciple recognizes:

“What I have attained would not have been possible without Him.”

5. Hanuman looking for Ram in the necklace

Gurudev compares the disciple's devotion to Hanuman's relationship with Ram.

He refers to the story of Hanuman looking for Ram within the necklace.

The underlying feeling is:

If Ram isn't there, what value does this have for me?

Likewise, the disciple wants his Guru constantly present in his awareness.

Not simply physically present.

Internally remembered.

This leads to a really interesting example.

6. The bank balance example Gurudev asks:

Suppose you have a huge bank balance.

Can you physically see all that money right now?

No.

Can you touch every note?

No.

But you know it's there.

And merely remembering:

“I have ₹3 billion.”

can produce a sense of:

  • security,
  • happiness,
  • confidence.

So Gurudev says:

Why do you think you need to physically see your Guru every time to experience joy?

Simply remember Him from within.

Remembering:

  • the Guru's state,
  • the Guru's virtues,
  • the Guru's compassion,
  • the Guru's benevolence,

can itself fill you with joy and enthusiasm.

“Don't be so crazy to see Him, just remember Him from within.”

That is a beautiful practical takeaway from the satsang.

7. The disciple's entire journey Gurudev then summarizes what actually happened:

1. A worthy disciple meets an Enlightened Guru.

↓

2. The Guru explains the Six Fundamental Truths.

↓

3. The Guru shows him the path.

↓

4. Through His proximity, vibrations and teachings, the disciple is awakened inwardly.

↓

5. He experiences samadhi / inner bliss.

↓

6. He attains unprecedented Self-realisation — apurva bhan.

↓

And now he experiences two things:

1. His own soul.

2. The immense benevolence of his Sadguru.

These become almost inseparable in his experience.

When he goes within and discovers himself, he simultaneously realizes:

“Who brought me here?”

And that produces the gratitude of Aho! Aho!

8. The Guru's presence can itself transform you

Gurudev makes an interesting distinction here.

We tend to think the primary benefit of a Guru is:

His teachings.

But Gurudev deliberately places teachings third.

He talks first about:

  • proximity
  • vibrations
  • then teachings

He gives Ramana Maharshi as an example.

Ramana Maharshi was not known for giving endless long discourses. Yet disciples experienced profound inner transformation simply through being in his proximity.

Gurudev's point is that with an Enlightened Being, communication isn't necessarily restricted to intellectual teaching.

There can be something transformative about His presence itself—for a worthy disciple.

9. But the key word is WORTHINESS

This becomes another major teaching.

Gurudev says that an Enlightened Guru may be present…

but an unworthy disciple may experience nothing.

The issue isn't necessarily:

“I need a better technique.”

Gurudev says people may attend 80–90 retreats and still be unable to control their anger.

That doesn't necessarily mean meditation itself is defective.

His line is essentially:

“I'm not saying the method is wrong. I'm saying you are wrong.”

Meaning the inner receptivity of the seeker matters enormously.

And then comes Gurudev's memorable statement:

“You'll reach only by the flight of worthiness.”

A little genuine worthiness can open inward doors that enormous amounts of mechanical practice may fail to open.

So this satsang isn't only praising the Guru.

It is also indirectly asking:

Am I becoming the kind of disciple capable of receiving what the Guru is giving?

10. King Shrenik and Anathi Muni — another “Aho!”

Gurudev then connects this verse with the Uttaradhyayan Sutra.

King Shrenik encounters Anathi Muni, sitting beneath a tree in deep meditation.

When Shrenik sees him, he is overwhelmed by the Muni's spiritual qualities.

And what comes out?

Again:

“Aho!”

Gurudev recalls lines praising:

  • his appearance,
  • tranquility,
  • forgiveness,
  • lack of greed,
  • detachment from pleasures.

The idea is that the internal virtues are almost radiating outward.

Shrenik isn't analytically evaluating Anathi Muni.

He is struck by what he witnesses.

Hence:

Aho! What tranquility! Aho! What forgiveness! Aho! What detachment!

Again, wonder becomes greater than language.

11. Priyabalaben / “Grandma” example

There is a nice side-example here too.

Gurudev asks an elderly devotee, around 82, to recite the verses.

She remembers approximately 2,000 verses of Uttaradhyayan Sutra by heart.

He jokingly compares that with devotees struggling to remember Atmasiddhi's 142 verses.

But Gurudev's deeper point comes afterward.

She would apparently use fasting days to revise the entire scripture.

Gurudev says both:

fasting + scriptural study

should ultimately serve one purpose:

Increasing awareness of the soul.

So again, external religious practices aren't isolated achievements.

Their value lies in where they direct your consciousness.

12. “Shri Sadguru” — the words themselves have meaning

Gurudev then breaks down the adjectives.

Shri

Shri means wealth.

But here Gurudev points toward spiritual wealth:

  • wealth of knowledge,
  • wealth of Self-experience.
Karunasindhu Karuna = compassion Sindhu = ocean

Therefore:

Ocean of compassion.

Apar

Boundless.

Limitless.

Beyond measurement.

So the disciple is essentially saying:

O my Enlightened Guru, You possess the wealth of Self-realisation and are a limitless ocean of compassion.

13. Why an OCEAN of compassion?

Gurudev asks us to visualize an actual ocean.

He remembers seeing the Pacific Ocean, including from New Zealand and from airplanes.

You look out…

and cannot see its end.

Its:

depth cannot be fathomed.

Its:

vastness cannot be comprehended.

And Gurudev says:

So is the Sadguru's compassion.

You cannot identify the point where His compassion ends.

That's why Karunasindhu is such a powerful word.

Not:

“Guru is compassionate.”

But:

Guru is an ocean of compassion whose depth and expanse I cannot measure. Pasted text(8)

14. The Guru's compassion is SELFLESS

Gurudev contrasts ordinary love with the Guru's compassion.

Human beings may say:

“I love you.”

But sometimes underneath that love is an expectation.

Eventually something happens against our wishes and we say:

“After everything I've done for you, you couldn't even do this much for me?”

And suddenly the hidden transaction becomes visible.

There was an expectation of return.

Gurudev says the Guru's compassion is different.

It is selfless.

The Guru's motive is not:

“What will this disciple give me?”

His concern is:

the disciple's spiritual welfare and liberation.

That is why the disciple finds the compassion apar — boundless.

15. Gurudev's own practice: remembering one favour at a time This is one of the most beautiful personal examples in the satsang.

Gurudev says that when he was younger, he would sometimes take:

“Aho! Aho! Shri Sadguru, Karunasindhu Apar…”

and do an entire mala/rosary of it.

With every repetition, he would remember one favour Param Krupalu Dev had done for him.

One bead.

One upkar.

Another bead.

Another upkar.

He says he would become overwhelmed with joy and tears.

And after finishing the entire mala, instead of thinking:

“Okay, I remembered everything,”

he would feel:

“There are still so many favours left unacknowledged.”

That's exactly what Apar Upkar means experientially.

The more you count…

the more you realize you cannot count.

16. What exactly has the Guru done?

Gurudev then explains why the disciple feels so indebted.

The Guru:

  • hand-holds the struggling soul,
  • takes care of him,
  • gives spiritual teachings,
  • reveals spiritual secrets,
  • shows the correct path,
  • generates enthusiasm to actually make the right effort,
  • and ultimately helps bring the disciple to Self-realisation.

Gurudev makes an important distinction:

Knowledge is one thing. Enthusiasm is another.

It isn't enough that the Guru tells you what is true.

He somehow makes you want to live it.

And ultimately, according to the disciple's experience, through His grace, blessings and compassion, the seemingly endless cycle of transmigration and sorrow can come to an end.

17. “Sadguru” becomes “Prabhu”

There is a tiny word change in the verse that Gurudev spends significant time on.

First:

“Aho! Aho! Shri Sadguru…”

But then:

“Aa pamar par PRABHU karyo…”

Why didn't Param Krupalu Dev simply repeat Guru?

A purely grammatical or “bookish” person might expect consistency:

Sadguru above → Guru below.

But Gurudev says the disciple's emotion is developing as he speaks.

By the second line:

Guru has become Prabhu.

Lord.

The disciple isn't sitting there choosing vocabulary intellectually.

His devotion is overflowing.

As Gurudev puts it:

The disciple becomes “pamar”; the Guru becomes “Prabhu.”

18. Gurudev's beautiful distinction: intellect, love, faith

Gurudev talks about how he refers to Param Krupalu Dev.

He essentially says:

Intellect says:
“Shrimadji.”

A respectful, formal designation.

Love says:
“Param Krupalu Dev.”
Faith says:
“Prabhu.”

That last word expresses something beyond intellectual respect.

It represents the disciple seeing the Guru as Lord.

Gurudev says that for the disciple, God and Guru are not experienced as two competing entities. Pasted text(8)

19. “My Guru is the Supreme Court”

Gurudev gives another memorable analogy:

“My Guru is the Supreme Court. There is no court higher than that.”

Meaning the disciple doesn't think:

“If Guru tells me something I don't like, I'll appeal somewhere else.”

There isn't another authority he keeps in reserve whenever Guru becomes inconvenient.

This is Gurudev's point about unshakeable faith.

Otherwise, the mind keeps moving:

Guru when Guru feels comforting → God when Guru corrects me → Guru again when I want blessings.

Gurudev says that isn't genuine devotion.

The disciple's refuge has become unwavering.

20. The Nemi example — remembering Guru even during pain

Gurudev then gives the striking example involving Nemi.

Gurudev was pressing his nail and causing him considerable pain.

Nemi closed his eyes and remembered Param Krupalu Dev, asking for strength.

Gurudev strongly corrected him.

Why?

The teaching Gurudev was trying to instill was:

Your Guru isn't only your refuge during happiness.

When the Guru Himself is putting you through something uncomfortable for your growth, don't mentally abandon Him and seek refuge elsewhere.

Remember your Guru in pain too.

This is about developing single-pointed, unwavering refuge, especially when the Guru's training becomes difficult.

21. Why Gurudev trained him so strictly

Gurudev then explains something important about strictness.

Nemi apparently jokes that newer children get pampered while:

“You roasted me like papad.”

Gurudev's response is essentially:

Because I wanted more virtues to emerge from you.

And importantly:

“Your faith was unshakeable, your devotion was unshakeable, so I could work on you.”

This completely changes how the strictness is framed in the satsang.

From the disciple's perspective, compassion does not always look like:

comfort.

Sometimes compassion looks like:

training.

Sometimes:

correction.

Sometimes:

being challenged.

Sometimes:

having one's ego exposed.

If the Guru's only agenda is your liberation, then even uncomfortable training can be understood by the disciple as upkar.

22. “Aa pamar par…” — look at the disciple's humility

Now Gurudev turns to the disciple himself.

The disciple has just become Self-realised.

Yet how does he describe himself?

Not:

“I am enlightened now.”

He says:

Pamar.

Meaning:

  • wretched,
  • unworthy,
  • worthless,
  • spiritually poor,
  • someone with no capacity of his own.

Gurudev points out the contrast:

Guru → Prabhu
Self → Pamar

The Guru becomes infinitely great in his eyes.

The self becomes smaller.

And Gurudev says such language can arise only from profound humility, where ego has begun to depart.

23. “You took all this trouble for ME?”

This is really what pamar is expressing.

The disciple looks at himself:

I was filled with flaws. I had attachments. I was entangled in worldly matters. My mind constantly ran toward family and worldly concerns. I had faith-delusion. I identified with the body.

And yet:

You bothered with me.

Not because You needed anything.

Not because You benefited.

Not because I deserved it.

But solely for:

my spiritual welfare.

my liberation.

That's why gratitude becomes overwhelming.

The disciple isn't merely saying:

“Thank You for teaching me.”

He's saying:

“Why would Someone of Your state take so much trouble for someone like me?” Pasted text(8)

24. Gurudev's “lift” analogy

Gurudev says the disciple was at the lowest rung of the ladder.

Then the Guru uplifted him.

And Gurudev starts thinking of an actual lift/elevator.

You enter the lift…

and it carries you upward.

His playful analogy becomes spiritually meaningful:

Take refuge in the lift.

The Guru uplifts the disciple from:

worldly mire

↓

faith-delusion

↓ body-identification

↓

attachment and entanglement

↓

all the way to:

Enlightenment.

And then the disciple looks back down and thinks:

“What was I… and what am I now?”

That comparison makes the Guru's benevolence impossible to ignore.

25. “What was I, and what am I now?”

This may be the most important gratitude practice in the entire satsang.

Gurudev says:

“When I think what I was and what I am, that is the moment I experience Your immense benevolence.”

That's the key.

To experience Guru-upkar, don't merely think abstractly:

“My Guru has done a lot for me.”

Actually compare:

What was I?

with

What am I now?

What was my thinking like?

What were my priorities?

What controlled me?

How much attachment did I have?

How reactive was I?

What did I understand spiritually?

What was my relationship with the Self?

And then:

What has changed because He entered my life?

The distance between those two versions becomes the measure of His upkar.

And then you realize:

It cannot actually be measured.

26. Why does the verse END with “Aho! Aho!” again?

This is beautiful.

The disciple begins:

Aho! Aho!

because he cannot find words.

Then he tries.

He manages:

Shri Sadguru Karunasindhu Apar Aa pamar par Prabhu karyo…

He has gathered some words.

But then he remembers more and more of what the Guru has done.

And again…

the words collapse.

So the verse ends exactly where it began:

“Aho! Aho! Upkar!”

The more he remembers the favours, the less adequate language becomes.

Gurudev suggests:

  • the first “Aho! Aho!” can be understood as amazement at the state/greatness of the Guru;
  • the second “Aho! Aho!” as amazement at His compassion and benevolence.

But he also says the exact interpretation isn't rigid—the important thing is the experience of awe, reverence and gratitude.

The deepest message I take from this satsang

The previous satsang ended with:

Moksh = Nijshuddhata.

The disciple finally understood and experienced what Gurudev had been explaining.

But this satsang answers:

What happens to your relationship with Guru when you actually realize what He has given you?

You don't become proud:

“Look what I attained.”

You become more humble.

Because the more clearly you see your transformation, the more clearly you see the hand that brought it about.

So there are almost two simultaneous discoveries:

“I discovered my Self.”

and

“I discovered what my Guru has done for me.”

That is why Gurudev explicitly says that when the disciple goes within, he experiences two things: the soul and the immense benevolence of the Enlightened Master.

And I think there's a very practical teaching here for our relationship with BAPA too:

We can spend our time thinking:

“I wish I could see Him.” “I wish I could be physically around Him more.” “I wish I could get more time with Him.”

But Gurudev specifically says don't depend only on physically seeing the Guru.

Remember Him.

Remember His state.

Remember His compassion.

And perhaps most importantly:

Remember His upkar.

One by one.

What was I before?

What did He introduce into my life?

What did He teach me?

What tendencies has He helped me recognize?

What direction did He give me?

What relationships did He bring me toward?

What has changed in the way I understand my goal?

What would my life look like if I had never met Him?

That is essentially Gurudev's own mala practice from this satsang: one bead, one remembered favour. And after an entire mala, realizing:

There are still so many left.

Then “Aho! Aho!” stops being something we're merely reciting from Atmasiddhi.

It starts becoming something we can feel.

And that's why Gurudev himself says near the middle of this satsang:

This is not a matter of notes making. It is something to feel.
