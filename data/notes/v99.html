Verse: 99 (Atmasiddhi Shastra) Theme of this verse: Bondage-path vs Liberation-path (Cause → Effect clarity) Core message: If you want liberation, stop walking on causes that create bondage; cultivate the inner state that destroys those causes.

1) Context: where Verse 99 sits in the Guru’s solution

You’re in the first group of the Guru’s 22-verse explanation (Instruction section).

  • Verse 98: Introduced the foundation:
    • Karmabhaav = ignorance (identifying with karmic manifestations)
    • Mokshbhaav = nijvas (abiding in the Self)
    • Darkness vs light (ignorance destroyed by knowledge-light)
  • Verse 99: Builds on that by giving a very clear diagnostic rule:
    • What leads to bondage is bondage-path.
    • What destroys the causes of bondage is liberation-path.

The speaker keeps emphasizing: Even if you forget everything, remember this verse—because it’s a universal, logic-based compass.

2) The “universally acceptable” framing (wrapper vs chocolate)

A big opening tone of this discourse:

A) This path is not sect-dependent

He says:

  • Whether you call it Shuddhatma, Brahma, Holy Spirit, Rooh, etc.—use whatever language you like.
  • “Wrapper can vary; chocolate is the same.”

Point being: The inner mechanics of bondage and freedom are universal—even if religions package them differently.

B) Why this matters (ties to doubt #3 from earlier)

The disciple’s earlier confusion was:

  • “Do I need Shwetambar? Digambar? This attire? That sect?”

Verse 99 implicitly answers:

  • Don’t get lost in wrappers.
  • Track causes of bondage and the state that removes them.

3) The verse’s two-line architecture (the whole verse in plain logic)

The satsang breaks Verse 99 into two perfectly logical statements:

Line 1: Bondage-path

“Je je karan bandhna, teh bandhno panth.”

Meaning:

Whatever are the causes of bondage, that itself is the path of bondage.

He even uses the road analogy:

  • A road that leads to Surat is “Surat road.”
  • A road that leads to bondage is “bondage road.”

Line 2: Liberation-path

“Te karan chhedak dasha, mokshpanth bhavant.”

Meaning:

The inner state that destroys those causes is the path of liberation—leading to bhavant (end of embodied wandering / end of transmigration).

So the verse is giving you:

  • What is wrong path? (bondage-causes active)
  • What is right path? (bondage-causes weakening/destroyed)

4) “Bhavant” — what result does mokshpanth lead to?

The speaker explains bhavant as:

  • end of embodied condition (no more repeated “bhav” changes)
  • cessation of birth-death cycles
  • end of transmigration

So the verse doesn’t just say “be spiritual.” It says: this is the direction that actually ends the cycle.

5) The practical instruction: first understand bondage if you want liberation

A key teaching line (very exam-worthy):

To understand the path of liberation, you must understand the path of bondage—and deliberately turn away from its causes.

This becomes a personal diagnostic tool:

  • If certain inner qualities are increasing → you are unknowingly walking the bondage-path.
  • If certain inner qualities are decreasing / getting destroyed → you are walking the liberation-path.

This is why the speaker calls it “two chapters of Tattvartha Sutra in two lines” (his way of saying: massive truth compressed).

6) The cause–effect logic (chilli / sweetener examples)

The speaker insists this is not “high philosophy only”—it’s plain logic:

General rule:

  • If a cause produces an effect:
    • Want the effect → engage the cause
    • Don’t want the effect → stop engaging the cause (or engage the opposite cause)

Example 1: Chilli → spicy dal

  • Want spicy? Put chilli.
  • Don’t want spicy? Don’t put chilli.
  • Merely staring at chilli won’t make dal spicy (he stresses engaging, not just “association”).

Example 2: Sweetness without sugar rise

  • Want sweet taste but not sugar spike?
    • Don’t add sugar; add sweetener (a cause that produces sweetness without that unwanted effect)

Spiritual translation:

  • Want liberation?
    • Stop feeding bondage-causes.
    • Cultivate destroyer-states (chhedak dasha).
  • Want bondage but also want peace? That contradiction won’t work.

7) The “root cause” emphasis (don’t trim branches; fix roots)

This is one of the most practical sections.

He says (in essence):

  • No use trimming branches if the roots are rotten.
  • If restlessness is rooted in attachment, then:
    • doing random activities (even “spiritual” ones) without touching attachment
    • won’t bring liberation-direction.

He gives direct examples:

  • “I have anger → I’ll do lots of scriptural study, but I won’t examine triggers.”
  • “I have indolence/irreverence → I won’t address it, but I want liberation.”

His point: Verse 99 forces honesty. It demands: “Name the cause. Work there.”

8) Five causes of bondage (he introduces a classic “five-factor” list)

In the latter half, he connects Verse 99 to a very structured Jain diagnostic model:

A) Five causes (bondage factors)

He gives them in Gujarati + English:

1. Mithyatva — wrong faith / wrong belief

2. Avirati — absence of restraint

3. Pramad — indolence / negligence / unawareness

4. Kashay — passions (anger, pride, deceit, greed; also attachment-aversion broadly)

5. Yog — activity of mind, speech, body (mann–vachan–kaya)

He asks people to repeat them in order—because this is meant to become a working checklist.

9) The “destroyers” (chhedak dasha) — five virtues that remove the five causes

Then he applies Verse 99’s second line:

“Te karan chhedak dasha…” If causes are five, destroyers/virtues also appear as five.

Cause → Destroyer (virtue)

1. Mithyatva (wrong faith) → Samyak Darshan / Samyak Shraddha (right faith)

2. Avirati (no restraint) → Virati (restraint / vows / inner self-control)

3. Pramad (indolence/unawareness) → Apramad (steady alertness; constant consistent awareness)

4. Kashay (passions) → Vitragta (dispassion)

5. Yog (outer activity flow) → Ayog (withdrawal inward; reduction of compulsive activity)

He stresses a nuance on Apramad:

  • It’s not “sleep less.”
  • It’s: after waking, be in awareness (inner alertness).

10) Samvar connection (scriptural name for “chhedak dasha”)

He then labels the set of destroyers as:

Samvar

Meaning:

  • stoppage of karmic influx
  • shutting the door where water is entering (cyclone / leakage analogy)

So Verse 99 is teaching:

  • bondage path = open doors (causes active)
  • liberation path = closing the doors (samvar = chhedak dasha)

11) How Verse 99 becomes a personal “satsang response” to restlessness

He says (paraphrase of his tone):

  • If you come and say, “I’m restless even though I do prayers/meditation/study,”
  • the response is not more activities first—
  • the response is Verse 99:

What are you engaging in that causes restlessness/bondage? Identify it. Reduce it. Replace it with destroyer-states.

This is the verse’s practical power:

  • It doesn’t let you hide behind “I’m doing so much.”
  • It asks: Are you addressing the actual causes?

12) What Verse 99 is not saying (important balance)

The verse does not say:

  • “Only one sect has the path.”
  • “Only one external practice liberates.”
  • “Do more and more rituals and you’re done.”

It says:

  • Bondage is causal.
  • Liberation is causal (destroying causes).
  • Your outer wrapper can vary, but your inner cause-work must be real.

13) One-page “study recap” (fast revision)

Verse 99 in two lines:

  • Bondage-path: causes of bondage = bondage road
  • Liberation-path: destroyer-state of those causes = liberation road → bhavant ends

Diagnostic rule:

  • Want liberation? Stop bondage-causes.
  • Don’t want bondage? Don’t live in bondage-causes.

Five causes (bondage):

1. Mithyatva

2. Avirati

3. Pramad

4. Kashay

5. Yog

Five destroyers (chhedak dasha / samvar-side virtues):

1. Samyak Darshan

2. Virati

3. Apramad

4. Vitragta

5. Ayog
