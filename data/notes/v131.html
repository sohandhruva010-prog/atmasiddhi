This is Verse 131 of the Upsamhar, and this is where Param Krupalu Dev begins one of the most important philosophical warnings in the conclusion: do not become one-sided in the Nishchay viewpoint.

Verse 130 established the foundation:

If you truly desire Parmarth, make Satya Purusharth—right effort. Don't use destiny, karma, time, etc. as excuses.

Now verses 131–136 will show specific ways a sincere spiritual aspirant can misunderstand a correct spiritual principle and therefore make the wrong spiritual effort. Gurudev says verses 131–132 form the first pair, dealing with the balance between Nishchay and Vyavhar. Pasted text(20261110-041448)

The verse is essentially:

“Nishchayvani sambhli, sadhan tajva no'y;
Nishchay rakhi lakshma, sadhan karva soy.”

Meaning:

Upon hearing statements about the Self from the absolute viewpoint, one should not abandon the means of attaining that state. Keeping the absolute truth as one's focus, one should continue practicing the appropriate spiritual means. Pasted text(20261110-041448)

Section 12 — Upsamhar
Verse 131: Keep the Goal in Sight, But Don't Abandon the Means

The entire verse can almost be remembered as:

Nishchay = destination
Sadhan/Vyavhar = vehicle

Know exactly where you're going.

But don't throw away the vehicle before you've arrived.

1. Verse 130 was the FOUNDATION for the next six verses Gurudev begins by connecting everything.

Verse 130 taught:

“Karo Satya Purusharth.”

Make the right effort.

But now an obvious question arises:

What exactly IS right effort?

Because even a sincere seeker can misunderstand spiritual philosophy.

Gurudev says this is especially dangerous:

The principle itself may be correct, but if understood incorrectly, your practice becomes incorrect.

So:

Correct principle + correct understanding → correct practice

but:

Correct principle + wrong understanding → wrong practice

And wrong practice can take you farther and farther from the destination. Pasted text(20261110-041448)

This is exactly why Gurudev called the Upsamhar a section about pitfalls and perils.

2. Verses 131–134 deal with Nishchay and Vyavhar

Gurudev says verses 131–134 point out the flaw of holding a one-sided belief.

Two viewpoints must be integrated:

Nishchay Nay

The absolute viewpoint.

Vyavhar Nay

The relative/practical viewpoint.

And Gurudev says:

Only an outlook integrating both is beneficial on the path of liberation.

Verse 131 specifically addresses someone who has become intoxicated with Nishchay alone and has therefore started moving away from the path. Pasted text(20261110-041448)

3. What does Nishchay say?

From the absolute viewpoint, I hear extraordinary statements about my true nature:

I am pure consciousness.

My inherent nature is blissful.

My nature is indestructible.

My true nature is like that of the Siddhas.

The soul does not eat.

The soul is inherently pure.

These statements are TRUE.

Gurudev is very clear about that.

The problem isn't Nishchay.

The problem is what I conclude from Nishchay.

I hear:

“I am inherently pure.”

Then conclude:

“So why do I need to purify myself?”

That's where the pitfall begins. Pasted text(20261110-041448)

4. “If I'm already pure, why meditate?”

This is the seeker's logic:

My nature is already pure.

So:

Why meditate?
Why perform austerities?
Why do bhakti?
Why study scripture?
Why do seva?
Why observe disciplines?
Why fast?

After all:

“The soul doesn't eat anyway.”

Gurudev humorously takes the logic further:

If the soul doesn't eat:

Why fast on Samvatsari?

If you eat at midnight:

The body is eating, not the soul!

😂

The statements sound philosophically sophisticated.

And each individual statement may contain scriptural language.

But the conclusion is wrong. Pasted text(20261110-041448)

5. This is exactly what Gurudev warned us about

Remember the previous satsang:

The excuse itself may not be false.

The misuse is false.

Verse 130:

Destiny exists → therefore why make effort?

Wrong conclusion.

Verse 131:

Soul is pure → therefore why practice?

Wrong conclusion.

This is the pattern Param Krupalu Dev is exposing:

A true principle can become spiritually dangerous when interpreted one-sidedly.

6. Gurudev's male/female discipline example

Gurudev gives a simple example.

From Nishchay:

We are all souls.

So someone might say:

“What male?”

“What female?”

“Those are only bodies.”

“Body isn't Self.”

Again:

True from the absolute viewpoint.

But then someone concludes:

“Therefore all practical disciplines involving male/female distinctions are meaningless.”

Now Nishchay has been used to cross Vyavhar disciplines.

Gurudev says that's misunderstanding the scripture. Pasted text(20261110-041448)

7. Gurudev's first visit to Deolali

This leads Gurudev into a personal story.

When he was around 12 years old, his maternal grandfather was suffering from tuberculosis, and circumstances led the family to stay in Deolali.

Gurudev didn't even initially know that the Shrimad Rajchandra Ashram was there.

He wandered around and was told children weren't supposed to disturb the spiritual atmosphere.

But then he went to behold Param Krupalu Dev and something awakened within him again.

He began sitting beneath a tree and meditating.

Eventually Jaisinghbhai noticed him, invited him in, sat him on his swing, gave him sweet lime and did satsang with him—telling him he could come and play as much as he wanted. Pasted text(20261110-041448)

Gurudev uses this memory partly to describe the practical discipline he noticed there—for example, separate places for men's and women's footwear.

His point:

Vyavhar has its place.

You can't erase every practical discipline by repeatedly saying:

“We're all souls.”

8. The SAME Acharya teaches both things

This is a very strong point.

Gurudev mentions Kundkundacharya.

Kundkundacharya teaches:

The soul does not eat.

But the same Kundkundacharya, in Niyamsaar, gives extensive rules and disciplines for monks.

So clearly:

“The soul doesn't eat”

does NOT mean:

“Therefore eating disciplines are meaningless.”

Otherwise the same Acharya would be contradicting Himself.

The real problem is:

We have failed to understand the different viewpoints from which the statements are made. Pasted text(20261110-041448)

9. You need multiple viewpoints to understand scripture correctly

Gurudev says:

“One needs to know the truth from multiple viewpoints to not misinterpret the scriptures.”

This directly continues Verse 128's teaching of Anekantvad.

A scripture may say:

From Nishchay:

The soul is eternally pure.

And:

From Vyavhar:

The embodied soul currently experiences impurity and must undertake spiritual practices.

Both can be true because they're describing reality from different viewpoints.

If I grab only one and reject the other:

I go astray. Pasted text(20261110-041448)

10. The core instruction of Verse 131

Param Krupalu Dev does NOT say:

Stop thinking about Nishchay.

Quite the opposite.

He says:

“Nishchay rakhi lakshma…”

Keep Nishchay in your focus.

Remember:

This is my true nature.

That is your destination.

But:

“…sadhan karva soy.”

Continue practicing the means required to manifest/realize that state.

So:

Goal = pure Self

while:

Path = appropriate spiritual practice. Pasted text(20261110-041448)

11. Awareness of Self + appropriate conduct

Gurudev summarizes the balance beautifully:

Awareness of the Self appropriate practices/conduct

=

progress toward liberation

Not:

practice without understanding the goal.

And not:

understanding the goal without practice.

Both.

This is the balance Param Krupalu Dev wants. Pasted text(20261110-041448)

12. The Dry Intellectualist — Shushkjnani

Gurudev connects this back to the beginning of Atmasiddhi.

Remember the Matarthi section?

One category was the:

Shushkjnani — dry intellectualist / impractical theorist.

This person studies scriptures describing the soul from Nishchay.

They learn beautiful phrases:

Atma is pure.

Atma is unblemished.

Atma is unbound.

Atma is non-attached.

Atma has infinite knowledge and power.

The problem?

They collect the WORDS without imbibing the ESSENCE. Pasted text(20261110-041448)

13. Then they start NEGATING spiritual practice

The dry intellectualist doesn't merely say:

“I personally don't need this.”

Eventually:

“Nobody needs this.”

They begin negating:

  • Yam — lifelong vows,
  • Niyam — temporary disciplines,
  • Saiyam — restraint,
  • Bhakti — devotion,
  • Tyag — renunciation,
  • Vairagya — detachment,
  • Swadhyay — scriptural/self-study,
  • Tapasya — austerity,

Why?

Because they have intellectually concluded:

“The Self is already pure.” Pasted text(20261110-041448)

14. The “auspicious bondage” argument

Gurudev gives an especially sophisticated version of this mistake.

Someone argues:

Correct.

↓

Auspicious activity creates auspicious feelings.

Correct.

↓

Auspicious feelings create auspicious karmic bondage.

Correct.

↓

Therefore:

“Don't do bhakti because it creates bondage.”

And Gurudev says:

Look carefully.

Every step before the conclusion used scriptural language.

But:

“Therefore don't do it”

was YOUR addition, not scripture's conclusion.

You used correct statements to manufacture a wrong spiritual practice. Pasted text(20261110-041448)

This is exactly the kind of subtle pitfall the Upsamhar is designed to expose.

15. Until Guru tells you you've outgrown the practice, don't decide yourself

Gurudev makes this very practical.

There may genuinely come a spiritual stage where certain gross practices are no longer necessary and the aspirant moves into subtler:

  • meditation,
  • Self-awareness,
  • abidance.

But:

Who decides that?

Not your ego.

Not your intellectual excitement.

Not:

“I read Samaysaar and now I'm beyond all this.”

Gurudev says essentially:

Until your Guru looks into your eyes and tells you that you no longer require a particular practice or need a subtler one, don't make that decision through swachhand — self-will. Pasted text(20261110-041448)

That connects directly to Verse 129:

Guru Ajna sam pathya nahi.

There is no greater regimen than Guru's Ajna.

Gurudev warns that without proper discernment:

  • lack of discipline,
  • unethical conduct.

A person can verbally proclaim:

“Hu Bhagwan Atma!”

That's the danger of spirituality becoming language rather than transformation. Pasted text(20261110-041448)

20. Now look at the SAME soul from Vyavhar

From the relative/practical viewpoint:

The embodied soul is currently:

  • bound by karma,
  • experiencing impure feelings,
  • acting,
  • receiving karmic fruits,
  • living in an impure state.

So Gurudev gives the beautiful distinction:

Your inherent nature may be pure.

But:

your present state is not yet pure.

And therefore:

The state must be purified. Pasted text(20261110-041448)

This one distinction resolves the entire confusion.

The deepest practical takeaway

I think Verse 131 can be reduced to one question:

Am I using spiritual truth to transform myself—or to excuse myself?

Because:

“I am the soul, not the body.”

can produce two completely different people.

Person 1

“I am the soul, not the body.”

Therefore:

Why should I care about discipline?

Why should I control desires?

Why should I follow rules?

Why should I fast?

Why should I do seva?

Why should I listen to anyone?

I'm already pure.

That becomes:

spiritualized self-will.

Person 2
“I am the pure soul.”

And therefore:

Why am I living like I'm merely the body?

Why am I so controlled by likes and dislikes?

Why does one person's opinion disturb me?

Why does pleasure control me?

Why does my ego dominate?

Why am I not experiencing the purity Gurudev says is my nature?

Now Nishchay creates:

inspiration for Purusharth.

Same statement.

Completely different result.

And I think that's exactly how we can test whether we've understood Nishchay correctly:

Does “I am pure” reduce my effort—or deepen it?

If it makes me complacent:

“I'm already pure, why do anything?”

then we're using Nishchay correctly.

Don't lower the goal because your present state is impure.

Keep the highest vision:

Sadhan gives the vehicle.

“Nishchay rakhi lakshma, sadhan karva soy.”

Keep the pure Self firmly in your vision—and keep practicing.
