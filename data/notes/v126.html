Absolutely. This lecture is the next step after the previous satsang, and the progression is important.

Previously, the disciple asked:

“Shu Prabhucharan kaney dharu?” What can I possibly offer at Your Lotus Feet?

He realized that every object is inferior to the soul, and even his awareness of the soul was given to him by Gurudev. So he concluded: I have nothing worthy enough to give You—therefore, I offer myself.

This lecture explains what “offering myself” actually means in practice:

“Aa dehadi aajthi, varto Prabhu adheen;
Das, das, das hu, teh Prabhuno deen.”

Essentially:

From this very moment, let this body and everything I consider mine act according to the Lord's command. I am the servant of the servant of Your servant.

Gurudev's focus is on surrender, ajna, seva, and above all, the melting of the “I.”

Atmasiddhi — “Aa Dehadi Aajthi”

1. “Aajthi” doesn't mean later today — it means NOW

Gurudev immediately focuses on:

Aajthi — from today.

But he says the disciple really means:

From this very moment.

Not:

“I'll think about it.”

Not:

“I'll start tomorrow.”

Not:

“Once my circumstances improve.”

The realization has happened now, so surrender begins now.

Later Gurudev returns strongly to this point:

If you go back to your room to think about it, the feeling might melt away.

The disciple doesn't want even a moment's delay.

I understood now → I change now.

Gurudev even says that once you have understood, delaying another moment becomes wrong.

This disciple has tremendous readiness.

2. “Aa dehadi” — what exactly is being offered?

Dehadi literally begins with the body etc.

But Gurudev expands it:

  • body,
  • mind,
  • senses,
  • intellect,
  • speech,
  • possessions,
  • everything the disciple considers “mine.”

So the offering from the previous verse is now made concrete.

Yesterday:

“Vartu Charanadheen.” I will live under Your command.

Today:

Everything I call mine is now available for Your purpose.

This is not merely emotional devotion.

It means the disciple's resources, abilities and life itself are reoriented.

3. “Prabhu Adheen” — according to HIS command

Gurudev again insists on the word Prabhu.

At this point, he says he cannot even translate it merely as “Guru,” because that would fail to capture the disciple's feeling.

The disciple experiences his Guru as Prabhu — Lord.

So:

Prabhu Adheen

means:

Let everything operate according to Your command.

This is the practical meaning of surrender.

Not simply:

“I love my Guru.”

But:

“My Guru's ajna determines how I live.”

That distinction becomes the central teaching of the entire lecture.

4. Atmasiddhi begins and ends in humility

Gurudev notices something beautiful about the whole scripture.

Atmasiddhi begins with humility.

And now, near its conclusion, it returns to extreme humility.

The disciple has attained Self-realisation.

Yet what is he saying?

Not:

“Look at my spiritual state.”

Instead:

“I am Your servant's servant's servant.”

So realization hasn't inflated him.

It has done the opposite.

The more he realizes the Self, the less ego remains.

Gurudev therefore sees vinay — humility running through Atmasiddhi from beginning to end. Pasted text(10)

5. “Teh Prabhuno” becomes “Aap Prabhuno”

There's a beautiful devotional detail here.

The original scripture says essentially:

“of that Lord.”

But Laghuraj Swami personalized it during Dev Vandan:

“Aap Prabhuno” — of YOU, Lord.

Gurudev loves this.

Why talk about Gurudev in the third person when you are praying directly to Him?

Not:

“May I become the servant of that Lord.”

But:

“I am YOUR servant.”

Gurudev says Atmasiddhi is teaching us how to make spiritual truths personal.

The scripture gives the generalized teaching.

But your heart has to turn:

“that Guru” → “MY Gurudev.”
“that Lord” → “YOU.”

6. Self-realisation is a complete metamorphosis

Gurudev then gives one of the central examples of the lecture:

Caterpillar → cocoon → butterfly

The disciple's transformation isn't:

“I learned some new spiritual concepts.”

His entire existence has changed.

During the Guru's explanation of:

  • nature of the soul,
  • characteristics of the soul,
  • bliss of the soul,
  • Six Fundamental Truths,

the disciple was slowly emerging from the cocoon.

Then Self-realisation happens.

The wings open.

The caterpillar becomes the butterfly.

Now it is ready to fly.

7. And who made this metamorphosis possible?

The disciple looks at this unbelievable change:

“What has happened to me?”

He was spiritually like a caterpillar since time immemorial.

Now he has wings.

So naturally he asks:

Who was central to this transformation?

His eyes turn toward:

Guru.

That's why these final verses contain so much gratitude and surrender.

We're merely observing the disciple's transformation.

He is experiencing it.

And because he directly experiences:

“I went from ignorance to Self-realisation because of my benevolent Guru,”

gratitude arises from the core of his being.

8. Guru becomes both mother AND father

Gurudev calls Self-realisation almost a:

second birth.

The transformation is so vast that it isn't like:

changing clothes.

It's like:

changing births.

And if this is the disciple's second birth, who are his spiritual parents?

Guru.

Gurudev deliberately says parents, plural.

Guru is:

  • mother,
  • father,
  • friend,
  • guide.

And he makes another important point:

Transformation cannot happen through pampering alone.

Sometimes a mother may be strict and father gentle; sometimes vice versa.

Likewise, Guru's compassion can appear in different forms.

If you simply pamper the caterpillar, Gurudev jokes, it isn't going to magically become a butterfly.

Transformation sometimes requires correction, discipline and training.

This directly connects with the Nemi example from the previous satsang.

Guru's compassion ≠ always making me comfortable.

Guru's compassion = doing what helps me transform.

9. The roller-coaster example — “Apurva” means unprecedented

The disciple describes his Self-realisation as apurva — unprecedented, something never experienced before.

Gurudev remembers his first trip to the U.S. as a child.

He had experienced simple rides like a Ferris wheel.

Then his uncle took him onto a roller coaster without properly telling him what was coming.

Suddenly:

zoom, zoom, zoom… 😂

And he comes out thinking:

What was THAT?!

Gurudev uses this simply to convey what unprecedented experience means.

You cannot completely understand it through prior reference because:

you have never experienced anything like it before.

Now magnify that infinitely beyond a roller coaster:

The disciple, who has lived in body-identification since beginningless time, suddenly experiences:

the True Self.

It is the spiritual equivalent of emerging from the cocoon and suddenly discovering:

I have wings.

10. Before Self-realisation: the emotional merry-go-round

Gurudev describes worldly existence as an endless emotional cycle:

sad in the morning, depressed in the afternoon, overwhelmed later, happy again the next day, then back into misery.

Round and round.

Like a merry-go-round.

Nothing fundamentally changes.

The disciple now realizes that ignorance of his true nature was behind his endless suffering.

With Self-realisation:

“I can see the end of worldly existence.”

The end has begun.

Gurudev describes it as:

“the beginning of the end.”

11. Self-realisation increases BOTH the value of Self and Guru

This is an important point.

We might assume:

Once I realize the soul, surely Guru becomes less necessary because now I've “got it.”

Gurudev shows the opposite.

After Self-realisation:

The value of the Self increases enormously.

But simultaneously:

The value of the Guru increases enormously.

Why?

Because now the disciple finally understands what Guru actually gave him.

Before experiencing the Self, he couldn't fully comprehend the magnitude of the Guru's gift.

After experiencing it:

“You brought me HERE?!”

So realization intensifies Guru-bhakti rather than diminishing it.

12. “Guru Brahma, Guru Vishnu…”

Gurudev explains the feeling of a Self-realised disciple:

My Guru is Supreme Power.

Call Him:

  • Brahma,
  • Vishnu,
  • Mahesh,

but ultimately:

Guru Sakshat Parabrahma.

Everything is encompassed in Guru.

Gurudev says this isn't merely a shlok he recites on Gurupurnima.

It reflects his own experience:

“I feel my Guru is the highest.”

13. “Give me the whole universe for my Guru — NO.”

Gurudev then demonstrates how valuable Guru becomes.

Suppose someone offers:

The entire universe

in exchange for Guru.

No.

What about:

All the pleasures of heaven?

Gurudev essentially calls them worthless by comparison.

No.

Then he takes it to the extreme:

What about the bliss of liberation itself?

Still:

Not if I have to give up my Guru in exchange.

He says, essentially:

I'm fine with my Guru. I don't want liberation if the price is exchanging Him.

This isn't a philosophical comparison of literal values so much as an expression of the disciple's absolute Guru-bhakti:

Nothing is valuable enough to make me exchange my refuge in Guru.

14. From immeasurable Upkar comes immeasurable Seva

Now Gurudev comes to das — servant.

The disciple has experienced:

immeasurable benevolence.

Therefore how could he respond with:

“I've done 37 hours of seva. That's enough.”

Gurudev gives a powerful principle:

If you can count your seva, you haven't understood the value of your Guru.

Why?

Because:

Guru's upkar = immeasurable

but

my seva = carefully measured?

The two feelings don't match.

If His benevolence cannot be measured, the disciple doesn't want to place a limit on his willingness to serve.

This doesn't mean physically working endlessly without limits. Gurudev's point here is the inner accounting mentality:

“I've already done enough.”

The disciple's heart doesn't approach Guru-bhakti as a transaction.

15. “Everything I call mine is Yours”

The disciple says:

From this very moment, I offer everything I consider mine.

Gurudev lists:

  • body,
  • mind,
  • senses,
  • intellect,
  • possessions,
  • everything.

And then he gives the story of Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj and Swami Samarth Ramdas. Pasted text(10)

16. Shivaji Maharaj offers his entire kingdom

According to the story Gurudev recounts, Swami Samarth Ramdas comes to Shivaji's court asking for alms.

Shivaji writes something on a piece of paper and offers it.

What did he write?

My entire kingdom.

Not:

₹1 crore.

Not:

₹5 crore.

Not:

some percentage.

Everything he could relatively call:

“mine.”

He placed it at Guru's Feet.

And what does Guru say?

“What am I going to do with your kingdom? I'm a monk!” 😂

17. Guru gives the kingdom BACK Swami Samarth Ramdas asks Shivaji:

“Will you do what I say?”

Shivaji's attitude is:

If You tell me to give my head, I'll give that too.

Then Guru essentially says:

This kingdom is mine now.

You administer it for me.

Rule it according to dharma.

And this is where the example becomes very important.

Outwardly, nothing necessarily changes.

Shivaji is still ruling.

He still sits on the throne.

He still administers the kingdom.

But inwardly:

Ownership has changed.

Before:

“My kingdom.”

After surrender:

“Guru's kingdom entrusted to me.”

He becomes a:

trustee.

18. This explains surrender beautifully

This is probably one of the most useful ideas in the lecture.

Surrender doesn't necessarily mean:

abandon your studies, work, family, possessions and responsibilities.

Shivaji didn't stop ruling.

Instead:

The ownership and purpose changed.

Similarly, the disciple still has:

  • body,
  • mind,
  • speech,
  • intellect,
  • possessions,
  • abilities,
  • responsibilities.

But now:

“These aren't primarily for my ego and pleasure. They are resources entrusted to me to use according to Guru's ajna.”

That's a much deeper understanding of:

“Aa dehadi aajthi, varto Prabhu adheen.”

19. King Kumarpal and Acharya Hemchandraji

Gurudev gives a similar example of King Kumarpal.

He recognizes:

It is because of Your grace that I have become king.

Therefore:

I place the kingdom at Your Lotus Feet.

Again, the disciple's response to receiving something isn't:

“Look what I achieved.”

It's:

“Whatever I have received is Yours.”

20. Gautamswami offers his 500 disciples

Gurudev then mentions Gautamswami.

He originally approaches Lord Mahavira with the ego/intention of defeating Him.

But after becoming convinced and surrendering, he offers even his 500 disciples.

His feeling becomes:

Whatever I called mine, I place at Your Feet.

Gurudev uses these examples not to say we should literally give away other people today—he even jokes about trying that now—but to show the spirit of complete surrender.

21. Same body. Same mind. Different USE.

This is a crucial line.

Before:

Body + mind + wealth → sensual enjoyment / ego / worldly purposes.

After:

Body + mind + wealth → Guru's purpose / ajna / seva.

Gurudev says:

“The use of body, mind, and wealth will still remain; but the usage has changed.”

That's surrender.

You don't necessarily receive a different body.

You don't receive another brain.

You don't necessarily abandon your resources.

Their direction changes.

22. “Das, das, das hu” — first meaning

Gurudev offers two interpretations of the three “das.”

The first:

Mind = Your servant
Speech = Your servant
Body = Your servant

Meaning:

I will THINK according to You.

I will TALK according to You.

I will ACT according to You.

Not only my physical seva.

My thoughts themselves should become aligned.

My words should become aligned.

My conduct should become aligned.

23. People should be able to SEE your Guru through you

This is one of Gurudev's strongest practical points.

He says your:

thinking should make someone behold your Guru.

Your:

talking should make someone behold your Guru.

Your:

behavior should make someone behold your Guru.

In other words:

Your Guru should be woven into your mind, speech and action.

Someone watching the disciple should almost think:

“His Guru must have taught him this.”

Whether:

  • speaking,
  • remaining silent,
  • doing seva,
  • acting,
  • interacting,

Guru's ajna should be visible through the disciple's life.

This is much deeper than wearing something associated with Guru or repeatedly talking about Him.

Your behavior becomes evidence of your Guru.

24. Otherwise, saying “I am Your servant” becomes empty

Gurudev becomes very direct here.

If every day we recite:

“Das, das, das hu…”

but in reality:

anger directs me,

arrogance directs me,

my preferences direct me,

my ego directs me, then Gurudev asks:

Where is Guru in your life?

Merely saying:

“He is in every pore of my body!”

doesn't prove anything.

The real test is:

Are my mind, speech and body actually becoming servants of His ajna?

25. “Das, das, das hu” — the SECOND meaning

This is even more intense.

Gurudev says it can mean:

“I am the servant of the servant of Your servant.”

Not merely:

I am Your servant.

But:

Let me serve someone who serves someone who serves You.

This represents extraordinary humility.

26. “If BAPA tells me directly, I'll do it…”

Gurudev gives an extremely practical example.

We sometimes think:

“If BAPA Himself tells me, I'll definitely do it.”

But:

“Why should I listen to this person?”

Gurudev says that misses the entire spirit of:

servant of the servant of the servant.

If Guru has entrusted someone with a responsibility and they give an instruction within that role, the disciple's ego shouldn't immediately say:

“Who are YOU to tell me?”

Because that reaction reveals:

I still find myself very significant.

This is a very practical application of humility in seva.

27. From capital “I” → small “i” → no “I”

Gurudev gives a fantastic progression:

Capital I

First, ego is huge:

I know. I did. I deserve. Why should I?

↓

small i

The ego becomes less important.

↓

No I

The self-willed identity melts.

Gurudev says:

“First, from the capital ‘I’ we need to come to a small ‘i’. And then don't use ‘I’.”

This is really the spiritual core of the lecture.

Because Prabhu Adheen and strong self-will cannot coexist.

The more:

“I want”

dominates,

the less:

“As You wish”

can operate.

28. Guru's greatness and my insignificance are two sides of one coin

Gurudev says true humility requires two realizations:

1. Experience the greatness of Guru.

2. Recognize the insignificance of one's ego.

These naturally go together.

You can't sincerely say:

“My Guru is unimaginably great…”

and simultaneously:

“…and I am also pretty amazing.”

Gurudev compares it to:

Mount Meru vs a pebble.

When Guru's greatness is truly experienced, the ego naturally becomes smaller.

29. This humility is NOT weakness This is important.

Gurudev clarifies that:

“I am servant of the servant of the servant”

does not arise because the disciple is:

  • helpless,
  • powerless,
  • forced,
  • socially conditioned,
  • compelled by a rule.

Nobody is telling him:

“If you want to stay here, you better behave like this.”

No.

It flows naturally from within.

Why?

Because he has experienced:

Guru's greatness + Guru's upkar + the smallness of his own ego.

So surrender becomes joyful rather than humiliating.

30. Guru may tolerate my ego. Liberation won't.

Gurudev ends with a very strong line.

The Guru may be:

  • compassionate,
  • patient,
  • understanding,
  • forgiving of mistakes,
  • willing to listen to our nonsense.

But:

Liberation will not tolerate the “I.”

Guru may tolerate your ego while helping you remove it.

But the final liberated state cannot coexist with that ego.

So ultimately:

the “I” has to melt.

Gurudev says this path requires:

ego melted + unwavering faith + utmost devotion.

The progression across these last three satsangs

These three lectures really form one continuous movement:

1. “Aho! Aho! Shri Sadguru…”

The disciple realizes:

Look at everything my Guru has done for me.

This creates:

GRATITUDE.

↓

2. “Shu Prabhucharan kaney dharu?”

He asks:

How could I possibly repay You?

Everything material is inferior to the soul.

And awareness of the soul itself came from Guru.

Therefore:

I have nothing worthy enough to offer.

So gratitude becomes:

SURRENDER.

↓

3. “Aa dehadi aajthi…”

Now he explains what surrender means:

From THIS moment, everything I consider mine will operate according to Your ajna.

My:

mind → Yours speech → Yours body → Yours abilities → Yours possessions → Yours

And:

I am happy even being the servant of the servant of Your servant.

So surrender becomes:

LIVED HUMILITY + AJNA + SEVA.

The biggest practical message

For me, Gurudev is making an important distinction between saying “I surrender to BAPA” and actually living surrendered.

It's easy to feel:

“BAPA, everything is Yours.”

But then everyday life comes.

Someone in seva tells me something I don't like.

“Who are they to tell me?”

Something doesn't happen according to my plan.

Anger.

Someone else gets recognition.

Jealousy.

I'm corrected.

Pride gets hurt.

I don't like an ajna.

“But I think…”

And suddenly Gurudev's question becomes extremely relevant:

Then where is Guru in my life?

Because this verse isn't primarily asking whether I can emotionally say:

“BAPA, I am Yours.”

It's asking whether my mind, speech and actions demonstrate it.

And that connects beautifully with your role in seva too: the test isn't merely how much seva you perform, but whether seva gradually reduces the “I” behind it. If an instruction comes through someone entrusted with responsibility, the immediate inner response doesn't need to be, “But BAPA didn't personally tell me.” The aspiration Gurudev presents is much deeper: servant of the servant of the servant.

That doesn't mean abandoning judgment or blindly following any random person's demand. Gurudev's specific framing is about someone appointed within Guru's work and the disciple's ego resisting simply because the instruction didn't come directly from Guru.

So perhaps the simplest self-check from this entire lecture is:

Who is directing me right now—Guru's ajna, or my “I”?

Because the disciple's journey is moving toward:

“What do I want?”

becoming less important,

and:

“What would YOU want from me?”

becoming more important.

That's “Aa dehadi aajthi, varto Prabhu adheen.”

And Gurudev isn't asking us to pretend we're already there. Near the end, he says something much more accessible:

“At least aspire for it.”

That may be the best place to begin.
