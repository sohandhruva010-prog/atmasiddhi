Two “segments” inside the Sixth Fundamental Truth

The speaker frames the Sixth Fundamental Truth (Means/Path of Liberation) as having two main parts:

Segment 1: Answers to the disciple’s arguments (earlier verses)

  • The disciple presented doubts/arguments.
  • The Guru answered them in sequence, “properly,” “wonderfully.”

Segment 2: The stages of liberation + concluding inspiration (this section)

  • Param Krupalu Dev lays out a step-by-step spiritual progression.
  • This progression is condensed into six verses: 108–113.
  • After that come five inspirational/closing verses (he says you can group them with this second segment if you like).

Helpful grouping option (as the speaker puts it)

  • Group A: Answering arguments (earlier)
  • Group B: Stages of liberation (108–113) + inspiration/closure (following verses)

He even calls it like “two yajnas” / “two groups” for clarity.

2) Roadmap of verses 108–113 (the “course”)

He gives a clean “curriculum map”:

  • 108: Preparatory stage — eligibility/worthiness (4 virtues)
  • 109: First samkit — first phase of right faith (vyavahār samkit)
  • 110: Second samkit — shuddh samkit (experiential faith / glimpses)
  • 111: Third samkit — parmārth samkit (supreme/steady right faith)
  • 112: Charitra — right conduct (progressive refinement)
  • 113: Kevaljnan — omniscience (end)

Key doctrinal logic he repeats

Everything “boils down” to mohaniya karma (deluding karma).

  • Liberation requires being karmaless, but practically:

1. the central battle is with mohaniya.

  • Mohaniya is of two kinds:

1. Darshan-moh (belief/faith-deluding)

2. Charitra-moh (conduct-deluding)

So the sequence makes sense:

  • First, right belief/right faith (darshan becomes clear)
  • Then, right conduct (charitra becomes purified)
  • Then completion: omniscience/liberation trajectory

That’s why:

  • 108–111 are heavily about darshan (faith)
  • 112 shifts focus to charitra (conduct)
  • 113 is the culmination

3) “How long will it take?” – the dream/awakening metaphor

He uses a striking line (and repeats its meaning):

  • A dream of a million years ends instantly when you awaken.
  • Likewise, delusion prevailing since infinity can vanish with awakening/enlightenment.

He applies it practically:

  • If someone asks: “How long from verse 108 to 113?”
  • Answer in spirit: the moment awakening happens, the “long time” collapses.

He links this to later verse themes too:

  • “karmas since infinite times… in how long can they be annihilated?”
  • same principle: awakening ends the “infinite dream.”

4) Why verses 108–109 are emphasized as “more important than” later verses

Burj Khalifa analogy: foundations

He compares spiritual ascent to the world’s tallest building:

  • Burj Khalifa: huge structure; must withstand storms, etc.
  • Your soul’s goal is omniscience—so:
    • the foundation must be extremely strong

Therefore he states bluntly:

108 and 109 are even more important than 110–113 because if the base is weak, the higher stages cannot stand.

What are those “foundations” (verse 108)?

He restates the 4 virtues (eligibility traits):

1. Passions have calmed down (subsidence/pacification)

2. Exclusive desire for liberation

3. Grief for embodiment / weariness of transmigration

4. Inner compassion

If these are weak:
  • you can do lots of worship, austerities, discourses, devotional singing…
  • but you still won’t reach 109 (and certainly not 110–113)

Practical instruction he gives the mind

He advises a clear “command” to oneself:

  • Don’t rush “in other directions”
  • First: prepare yourself as a true aspirant / true seeker

5) “Jignasu” — a memorable breakdown (ji-gna-su)

He shares a personal insight on the word jignasu:

  • Ji = jiva (soul) → also linked to sat (existence)
  • Gna = jnan (knowledge) → also linked to chit (consciousness)
  • Su = sukh (happiness) → also linked to anand (bliss)

So ji-gna-su points to:

  • sat–chit–anand nature (existence–consciousness–bliss)

But he immediately grounds it:

“How do you become a jignasu?” By cultivating the same 4 virtues of verse 108.

He also clarifies:

  • what must be pacified are demonic/animal instincts (not “divine instincts”).

6) Bridge to Jay Viyaray Sutra: the same 4 virtues in prayer-language

He says these virtues are universal and timeless, and illustrates that with Jay Viyaray (described as composed ~2400 years ago by a Gandhar Bhagwan).

He lists four terms from Jay Viyaray and maps them directly onto Atmasiddhi’s 4 virtues:

(1) Bhavanivveo (bhav no nirved)

  • “O Lord, grant me weariness of embodiment”
  • He highlights a subtle point:
    • You may detect ego/lust/attachment…
    • but you may not feel tired of it
    • and without tiredness, practice doesn’t become intensely sincere.

Illness analogy: People get tired of fever/headache quickly—so why not get tired of endless birth-death cycling?

(2) Magganusaria

  • “May my intellect align with the path You have shown”
  • He links this with:
    • reducing violent outbursts of likes/dislikes
    • “You are my direction and intellect” (Tu gati, tu mati…)

He explicitly says:

  • this corresponds strongly with subsidence of passions (virtue #1)

(3) Itthafalsiddhi

  • “May I be blessed with the desired fruit”
  • Desired fruit = only liberation
  • Also: “May I not develop desires opposite to liberation”
  • He states: you get matra moksh-abhilash (exclusive desire for liberation) by grace + inner direction.

(4) Paratthakaranam cha

  • benevolence / helping others / inner compassion
  • He links this to antar daya (inner compassion)

Instruction he gives: Whenever you recite Jay Viyaray, remember Atmasiddhi; and when you study Atmasiddhi, remember Jay Viyaray—because Enlightened Ones share the same essence.

7) Inner compassion as a “non-optional way of life”

Three “debts” (roon) we must repay

He frames service/benevolence through the lens of debt-repayment:

1. Pitru roon – debt to parents (their benevolence)

2. Guru roon – debt to the Guru

3. Vishwa roon – debt to the world/society

So:

  • Helping others is not a “hobby.”
  • It should be a way of life, like repaying a loan.

Key ethical correction: don’t boast about service

He gives a blunt example:

  • If you borrowed money and repaid it, you can’t boast.
  • Similarly, doing charity/service is “giving back,” not ego material.

Mango seed story: benevolence across generations

Grandfather plants a mango seed though he may not live to enjoy it. Message:

  • The mangoes we enjoy now were planted by someone before us.
  • Planting now = repaying the loan forward.

This is paratthakaranam cha in action.

8) Strong critique of selfishness in daily life

He says many people run their day with two questions:

1. “Why should I care?”

2. “How will it affect me?”

He calls it the “stench” / foul smell of selfishness.

Compassion in action example (Ashwin’s illness)

He describes checking if someone needs medicine, calling them, caring even if you are busy. Point:

  • Inner compassion means: people around me also remain comfortable/happy.

9) “Don’t quit seva in the name of spirituality” — integrating service + sadhana

He addresses a common pattern:

  • People do seva, then suddenly say: “Now I’ll stop seva and do spiritual practice.”

He challenges the hidden cause:

  • Usually, ego got hurt or expectations weren’t satisfied.
  • The excuse can be “sweet,” but the root is often selfishness.

His integration formula

You have:

  • Mind, speech, body → use for seva
  • Awareness → use for spiritual practice

So doing both together should not be a conflict.

10) Virtues should bloom as you rise, not dry up

He insists:

  • As you progress, virtues should not be lost.
  • They should bloom more.

Mahavira example: compassion at the peak

He cites Bhagwan Mahavira’s last days:

  • Started discourse on Dhanteras
  • Continued for “16 prahar” (~two days)
  • Attained nirvana on Diwali night

Conclusion:

  • Compassion that begins in the seeker stage
  • is at its peak in omniscience—not absent.

11) Haribhadrasuri story: why “calm passions” is non-negotiable

He tells the story to show:

  • even a highly advanced monk-scholar can have a sudden surge of intense passion if awareness slips.

Main arc of the story (as given)

  • Haribhadrasuri: brilliant scholar → became a Jain monk → advanced quickly
  • Two nephews studying Buddhism in disguise; one is killed
  • Hearing it, he becomes furious and proposes:
    • debate all monks in that monastery (1444)
    • condition: loser will be fried alive
  • Immediately realizes: “What have I done?” seeks Guru → asks for prayaschit (atonement)

The prayaschit he receives (very severe)

Because he harbored violent intent toward 1444, he must compose:

  • 1444 scriptures (granth), not just verses.

He composes 1443, but is dying with one left. Guru allows a substitute:

  • compose a thoi (hymn/stotra) of four stanzas

1. dedicated to a Bhagwan/Sadguru

2. 24 Tirthankars

3. reverence for scriptures

4. Shasan Devi

He references the hymn beginning “Sansar davanal…”, and notes a ritual detail:

  • in pratikraman, certain parts are recited in a specific manner because of its origin.

Why this story matters in this discourse

It reinforces verse 108’s first virtue:

  • pacify passions early
  • because passions can surge even later if the foundation isn’t deeply stable

He sums it up sharply:

  • If an Acharya-level monk can have such passion,
  • then ordinary householders are “living in the house of passions.”

12) Transition into verse 109: the “romantic story” of liberation

He now shifts “into the mood of the verse.”

Core idea:

  • If someone has the four virtues (108),
  • and comes into Sadguru association,
  • and absorbs (not just hears) the teachings,
  • then they progress swiftly through:
    • first samkit → second → third → conduct → omniscience.

He emphasizes:

  • “absorbing” is a step beyond “listening.”

13) The art of listening — “Shravak” as a spiritual category

He says:

  • We think public speaking is an art,
  • but listening is also an art.

He mentions:

  • there are “10 types of listening” (he doesn’t list all, but names examples like):
    • discriminative listening
    • judgmental listening
    • compassionate listening

Jain term: Shravak

He says “shravak” (a Jain term) implies:

  • one who has the art of listening
  • no parallel “running” in the mind
  • total absorption in Guru’s words

Gautamswami listening example (very important)

He says:

  • The Lord spoke three words:
    • “Upanei va, Vigamei va, Dhuvei va” (creation, destruction, permanence)
  • From this, the Dwadashangi (12 canons) was composed.

Point:

  • their listening was so deep that essence expanded into scripture.

14) “Absorbing” as pregnancy — and the risk of “abortion”

He uses a provocative metaphor:

  • When you truly absorb teachings, it is like becoming pregnant with them.
  • If you can’t retain the state, it’s like an abortion—the inspiration doesn’t mature.

But:

  • keep trying again and again,
  • eventually the “baby” of Self-realisation is born.

He even adds:

  • what if “twins” are born?
    • Atmajnan (Self-realisation)
    • Vitragta (dispassion)

So the goal is:

  • not superficial inspiration that dries quickly,
  • but retained absorption that matures into lived realization.

15) Verse 109: the key line and its step-by-step meaning

He explicitly quotes/works through the verse wording:

“Te jignasu jivne thay Sadgurubodh, to pamey samkitne, varte antarshodh.”

Word-by-word flow

A) “Te jignasu jivne”

“That truth-seeking soul…”
  • “Te” refers back to the one from verse 108—endowed with the 4 virtues.

B) “Thay Sadgurubodh / Sadguru yog”

“…receives the Sadguru’s instruction / gets association and guidance…”

He expands what happens around Sadguru in three movements:

1. Association

2. Recognition (by virtues, not appearance)

3. Surrender

He also lists “recognition signs” (virtues of Sadguru as he says):

  • Enlightenment
  • Equanimity
  • living according to past karmas
  • unique speech
  • mastery over scriptures (he notes “this takes us back to verse 10”)

He also mentions the “4 I’s” of Sadguru:

  • Initiates
  • Inspires
  • Instructs
  • Inspects

C) “To pamey samkitne”

“…then attains samkit (right belief/right faith)…”

He clarifies:

  • This is the first phase of right faith:
    • vyavahar samkit
    • also referred to as shraddha samkit (faith-based)

Key property: Faith and doubt cannot coexist:

  • If you have real samkit (in this sense), you’re free of doubts regarding the Guru and his teachings,
  • but you may not yet have direct experience of the pure Self.

America example (used to explain vyavahar samkit)

He gives a relatable analogy:

  • Someone describes America in detail.
  • You trust their experience.
  • You haven’t experienced America yourself,
  • yet you become doubtless enough to “pack your bags.”

That = vyavahar samkit:

  • conviction based on the Sadguru’s experience,
  • preparing you for verse 110, where experiential faith arises.

He adds a key link:

  • Faith is “vision”; then legs begin moving = conduct begins.

D) “Varte antarshodh”

“…and lives in antarshodh.”

He insists antarshodh has two meanings, both valid:

1. Inner quest / inner search

  • turning the search from outside to within

2. Inner purification

  • “shodh” also means purification (shodhan)

He says they’re not contradictory; they merge:

  • inner search naturally requires inner purification,
  • so he “fuses” them as: embarking on an inner journey

16) What does “antarshodh” practically include?

He compresses it into three practices:

1. Introspection

2. Contemplation

3. Meditation

He jokes he previously spent 3+ hours just on “antarshodh,” but here he gives the core structure.

16.1 Introspection = Detection → Negation → Substitution

He says: three words and “you’re finished.”

1. Detection

  • detect your flaw clearly

2. Negation

  • do not justify it
  • do not blame circumstances
  • do not call it “just a phase”
  • own it as a flaw

3. Substitution

  • replace with virtues (inner cultivation)

He uses a sharp metaphor:

  • earlier you were an exterior decorator (rituals, outer activity, charity as “outer polish”)
  • now become an interior decorator (virtues, inner transformation)

16.2 Contemplation = Chintan → Smaran → Manan

He names the Gujarati idea of suvicharna (right thinking arising).

Contemplation becomes possible when the mind is somewhat purified:

  • disturbed/fatigued mind can’t focus
  • detection/negation/substitution clears enough space for real contemplation

Then:

1. Chintan Reasoning / analyzing

2. Smaran Recalling / remembering teachings and reinforcing them

3. Manan Repeating / chanting internally (identity-remembrance)

He gives examples of manan:

  • “I am the soul, not the body…”
  • reinforcing Sadguru’s essence as truth
  • steady repetition that reshapes identity

16.3 Meditation: from mechanical to absorbed awareness

He describes meditation as:

  • focusing more and more on the pure nature of the Soul (the eternal), not merely on changing states.

Process:

  • starts mechanical
  • gradually:
    • awareness increases
    • delusion/distraction decreases
  • result:
    • you develop worthiness for the second samkit (experiential glimpses)

He distinguishes:

  • verse 108: worthiness to begin the path
  • antarshodh practice: worthiness to experience (towards verse 110)

17) Practical “study summary” of this entire video

The central chain of verse 109 (as taught)

4 virtues (108) → Sadguru association + absorption → first samkit (faith without doubt) → antarshodh (inner journey) → readiness for experiential faith (110)

The strongest recurring warning

If foundation (108–109) is weak:

  • higher stages won’t happen, regardless of external religious activity.

The strongest recurring encouragement

If these foundations are present and you truly absorb:

  • progression becomes “slide-like” (effortless in a certain way).
