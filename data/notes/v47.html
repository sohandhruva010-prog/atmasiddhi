2) The disciple’s “absurd logic” (core psychological problem)

He names the disciple’s trap very clearly:

  • The disciple’s logic is:
    • “If it exists, it should be seen.”
    • “If I cannot see it, it does not exist (or I hesitate to accept it).”
  • He calls this absurdity of logic:
    • Existence is being equated with visibility/tangibility.

What will Sadguru do (preview)?

  • Sadguru will pull him out of this trap and make him see:
    • Senses are keys—useful keys,
    • but not the key to every door.
  • So the disciple will learn:
    • “Senses can reveal some realities, but not all realities.”

4) Verse 47: the disciple’s 3rd argument (the actual line + meaning)

He reads and explains:

“Vali jo ātmā hoy to, janāy te nahi kem? Ghaṭ paṭ ādi jem…”

Word-by-word flow as he explains

  • Vali jo… = “And if… (now my next point)”
  • Ātmā hoy to… = “If the soul exists…”
  • Janāy te nahi kem? = “Why is it not known/knowable?”
  • Ghaṭ-paṭ ādi jem = “Like pot, cloth, etc.”

What the disciple is trying to say (in simple logic)

  • If something exists as a substance, it should be knowable/known.
  • Pot/cloth are known → therefore we accept they exist.
  • So he argues:
    • “If soul exists, why isn’t it known like pot/cloth?”
    • “Because it isn’t known, it probably doesn’t exist.”

He compresses the 3 arguments in short (his own recap style):

1. Not seen / not sensed (45)

2. Just another name for body/senses/breath (46)

3. If it exists it should be knowable like tangible objects (47)

5) The hidden demand inside the disciple’s argument

He points out the real “confusion”:

  • The disciple wants to perceive soul the same way he perceives tangible objects.
  • Meaning:
    • “If soul exists, it must be matter-like.”
    • “It must have tangibility, visibility.”

So the disciple’s mindset is:

  • “I don’t mind accepting soul, but only if it is tangible.”
  • If it is not tangible → he refuses to accept existence.

6) The “address + visibility” point (location + tangibility)

He adds a powerful framing:

To believe a “substance,” the disciple demands two things:

1. Location / Address (Where is it?)

2. Visibility / Tangibility (Can I perceive it through senses?)

He says:

  • Soul (as Sadguru speaks of it) has:
    • no location known (address not known),
    • no visibility/tangibility (not caught by senses),
  • So the disciple says:
    • “Why even talk about existence, let alone nature/powers?”

This is why the disciple is stuck at:

  • “First prove existence; then talk about nature.”

7) What the disciple is missing: a lot exists beyond tangibility

He “opens the frame”:

  • Many things exist beyond the realm of tangibility.
  • He gives a Jain metaphysics reference:
    • Jain view speaks of six substances.
    • Matter (pudgal) is only one.
    • The others are not tangible/visible.
  • He connects it to our schooling:
    • We were taught “matter” (solid/liquid/gas) and that becomes our whole world.
  • He clarifies:
    • Spirituality is not “anti-matter.”
    • Jainism accepts matter as real, but not as the only reality.

So Sadguru’s job becomes:

  • First: expand the disciple’s notion of “what can exist”
  • Then: show signs (endhan) of soul without demanding crude tangibility.

10) The “puncture” section: breaking the obsession with tangibility

He says he must “puncture” the audience a bit because people are getting convinced by the disciple.

Story 1: Old Master under a tree vs young scholar charging fees

  • Old Master gives knowledge freely, out of compassion.
  • Young scholar thinks:
    • “Free things lose value, you must charge.”
  • He sets up a business and charges for knowledge.
  • Old Master comes and says:
    • “Teach me.”
  • Young scholar reads from papers, speaks words, finishes.
  • Old Master leaves.
  • Young scholar asks:
    • “Where are my fees?”
  • Old Master replies (the punch):
    • “Fees for what? Show me the knowledge.”
    • “Can I see knowledge? Can I touch it?”
    • “If it’s not tangible, why should I pay?”
Meaning behind it:
  • The young scholar’s own system collapses.
  • Because he insists:
    • only tangible things are real/valuable,
  • but knowledge is not tangible.

He makes the distinction:

  • Words ≠ knowledge
    • Words can exist without knowledge.
    • Knowledge can exist without words (silence can transmit).

So: If you accept “knowledge” exists though it’s intangible, why do you become rigid only about soul?

Story 2: “Did you see anger?”

He gives a sharp example:

  • If he throws a clock in anger, you’ll say:
    • “Bapa, what I saw was not good.”
  • He asks:
    • “What did you see?”
    • You saw behaviour, eyes, words, body movement.
    • But did you see anger itself?
  • If someone insists:
    • “If I can’t see it, it doesn’t exist,”
    • then they must say:
      • “Anger doesn’t exist.”
  • He says this kind of example is discussed in philosophy (he attributes this style to Dr. Radhakrishnan).
Meaning:
  • Inner realities are not always “seen” as objects.
  • Yet we know they exist by their manifestation.

Story 3: “Show me your intelligence”

He pushes it further:

  • “Show me intelligence.”
  • If you can’t show it tangibly, do you deny intelligence exists?
Meaning:
  • You already accept many non-tangible realities.
  • So the “tangible-only” rule is selective and inconsistent.

11) Back to Verse 47: why “ghat–pat” (pot–cloth) is used

He explains why scripture debates use these words.

  • In Indian philosophical debate language:
    • Ghaṭ (pot) and paṭ (cloth) are standard placeholders for all tangible objects.
  • Why?
    • Instead of listing thousands of examples, they say “ghat-pat” and it covers “the whole tangible world.”

He also praises Param Krupalu Dev here:

  • He shows Shrimad’s mastery of:
    • Self-experience (main),
    • Scriptures,
    • The disciple’s mental frame (how the student thinks).
  • That’s why he can “clean-bowl” the disciple:
    • by asking the right questions at the right pressure point.

12) Long digression: his own study of logic (Nyāya) and why it matters here

He shares personal background to show “ghat–pat” is a logic-term:

  • He studied logic for around 1.5 years:

  • He would read Sanskrit sutras, the teacher would expand.
  • Teacher was so intense he even instructed his mother:

  • He jokes:

  • He studied:

    • Tarka (logic),
    • Tarka-sangraha,
    • Navya-nyāya (Gangeśa of Mithila),
    • Modern logic (Bertrand Russell).

He explains the word Nyāya:

  • Bollywood “nyāya chahiye” = “justice”
  • But philosophically:
    • Nyāya = right judgment / right conclusion
  • And Nyāya sutras teach:
    • rules to reason properly, convince properly.
Meaning behind this whole segment:
  • Verse 47 is not random.
  • It is structured like a trained debater’s point:
    • “If it exists, it should be knowable.”
  • Shrimad is fully aware of classical debate structure.

13) Why pot–cloth specifically? (his professor’s answers)

He says: people today don’t even see “pots” in rooms, so why use “pot–cloth”?

He explains what he learned:

Answer 1 (he says it’s likely just to “shut him up”)

  • Because it rhymes: ghat–pat.
  • Easier to memorize in times when everything was oral/memorized.

Answer 2 (he finds more acceptable)

  • Village life realities:
    • A pot of water was essential.
    • A cloth was constantly used:
      • to filter water,
      • to cover the pot,
      • to keep water cool (cloth around/over pot).
  • So these were the most “eye-hitting” everyday tangible objects.
Meaning:
  • “Ghat–pat” is not poetic decoration.
  • It is a practical shorthand: “all tangible objects.”
