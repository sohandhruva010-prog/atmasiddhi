He reminds us:

  • Verse 43 → just named the Six Fundamentals (shatpad):

1. Soul exists

2. Soul is eternal

3. Soul is doer of karma

4. Soul is endurer of karma

5. Moksh (liberation) exists

6. There is a moksh-upāy (path of liberation), which is true dharma

Now, before launching into the detailed Sadguru–disciple debate from verse 45, Param Krupalu Dev has composed one more introductory verse:

Verse 44 – Introduction to the main subject of Atmasiddhi.

He distinguishes:

  • Intro to entire text: 23 verses (Upodghāt section).
  • Intro to main subject (shatpad): 2 verses (43 & 44), called “Śatpad-Nāmkathan”.

Verse 44 answers:

1. What is going to be said (content)?

2. Why it is being said (purpose)?

The “why” is especially important here.

2. The verse itself – rough sense

The verse says (in essence):

Śatsthanak saṅkṣepmā, śatdarshan pan teh;
Samjāva parmarathne, kahyā jñānie eh.

He unpacks word by word.

3. First half: “Śatsthanak saṅkṣepmā, śatdarshan pan teh”

3.1 “Śatsthanak saṅkṣepmā…”

  • Śat – six
  • Sthānak – standings / positions / aphorisms (same as “pada”)
    • He notes:
      • “Aphorism” is a heavy word,
      • If it’s too awkward, think “six fundamental truths” (not “six rules”).
  • Saṅkṣepmā – briefly.

So:

“The six aphorisms (six fundamentals) are stated here briefly.”

He notes with a smile:

  • According to Parm Krupalu Dev:
    • Even the entire dialogue from verse 45 to 118 is “brief”!
  • He, on the other hand:
    • Spent 6–8 years researching this for his thesis,
    • Referring to 500–600 books,
    • He had one core reference book during those days:
      • A brown hardbound volume he clung to more than his own life.

He jokes:

“I could part with my soul, but not with that book!”

This shows:

  • How extraordinarily concentrated Param Krupalu Dev’s expression is.

3.2 “Śatdarshan pan teh…”

  • Śatdarshan – “six philosophies” / “six schools of thought”.
    • Darshan here means philosophy, not just “seeing”.
  • Pan teh – “also include them”.

So:

“This discussion will also contain the six major philosophies – their essence, their viewpoints.”

Therefore, the upcoming Guru–disciple dialogue will:

  • Explain the six fundamentals (śatsthanak),
  • And also include a critical appreciation of the six Indian philosophical systems (śatdarshan).

He lists the six philosophies later; we’ll summarise them below.

4. Second half: “Samjāva parmarathne, kahyā jñānie eh”

4.1 “Samjāva parmarathne…”

  • Samjāva – “to make (us) understand”.
  • Parmarath – “supreme truth / ultimate reality”.

So:

“All this is explained in order to make us understand the supreme truth.”

The purpose is:

  • Not to show off scriptural knowledge,
  • Not to do philosophy for pastime,
  • Not to get lost in cleverness.

But:

“To understand the ultimate reality for the welfare of the soul.”

4.2 “Kahyā jñānie eh…”

  • Kahyā ew – “have been stated thus”.
  • Jñāni – Enlightened Ones (not merely scholars).

So:

“These truths and philosophical analyses have been stated by the Enlightened Ones to help us understand the supreme truth.”

He emphasises:

  • Param Krupalu Dev is not claiming:

“I invented these 6 truths or 6 philosophies.”

  • He is restating:
    • What Jinas, Acharyas, Upādhyāys, Jñānis have already expounded,
    • For the purpose of:
      • Clarifying truth,
      • Freeing the seeker from confusion.

Short meaning (as he gives it):

“These six aphorisms are stated here briefly. They also implicitly include the six systems of philosophy. The Enlightened Ones have expounded these truths to help us understand the supreme reality.”

5. “Critical appreciation” of the six philosophies

In the bracketed summary, he uses a very carefully chosen phrase:

“Critical appreciation of the six systems of philosophy.”

He says:

  • It took him over an hour and a half of thought to crystallise these two words.

Because:

  • He wants to express both:

1. Critical – evaluating, analysing:

  • Not saying “everyone is right in every way.”
  • Not appeasing.
  • E.g. “From this specific viewpoint, this philosophy is 100% correct. But if it claims that viewpoint to be the whole of truth, then it becomes limited.”

2. Appreciation – acknowledging what’s valid, beautiful:

  • Not just dismissing other views.
  • Not sectarianly shouting:

“Only Jainism is right; everyone else is wrong.”

So:

“Critical appreciation” = Recognising the truth in each viewpoint, while also seeing where it stops short of the full picture.

The purpose is:

  • Integration:
    • To see how partial truths can be reconciled,
    • To see why Jainism is called:

Anekāntvād – explaining truth from multiple viewpoints, because truth is multi-faceted.

Not:

  • Blind appeasement,
  • Nor arrogant rejection.

6. The six systems of philosophy (Śatdarshan)

He then names the six major Indian philosophical schools whose views will be implicitly addressed under each fundamental:

1. Chārvāka – pure materialism

  • Only matter is real,
  • No soul, no afterlife, no liberation.

2. Jainism

3. Buddhism

4. Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika (paired)

  • Logic & atomism, Veda-accepting.

5. Sāṅkhya–Yoga (paired)

  • Dualism (Purusha–Prakriti) & classical yoga path.

6. Pūrva Mīmāṃsā & Uttar Mīmāṃsā (Vedānta) (paired)

  • Former focuses on ritual; latter on Brahman / Self.

He groups them:

  • Those not treating Vedas as ultimate authority:
    • Chārvāka, Jainism, Buddhism (Jain & Buddhist respect Vedas but don’t treat as final authority).
  • Those accepting Vedas as ultimate authority:
    • Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika,
    • Sāṅkhya–Yoga,
    • Pūrva Mīmāṃsā–Uttar Mīmāṃsā.

The six fundamental questions (existence of soul, its eternality, doer, endurer, liberation, path) are like axes on which:

  • Every school builds its viewpoint:
    • “Does soul exist?”
    • “Is soul eternal?”
    • “Who is doer?”
    • “Is liberation possible?” etc.

Thus:

Any discussion of the Six Fundamentals inevitably involves the six philosophies – hence śatdarshan pan teh.

And Param Krupalu Dev says:

“I’ve woven the essence of these six philosophies into this Guru–disciple discussion for you.”

7. Why this matters: the novice seeker lost in the maze

He paints the predicament of a modern seeker:

  • Different philosophies say radically different things:
    • One says: no soul; only matter (Chārvāka).
    • Another: soul exists but not eternal.
    • Another: you are not the doer; God does everything.
    • Another: someone else decides consequences; you only suffer.
    • Another: doer & sufferer, but no liberation possible; just heaven/hell.
    • Many religions:
      • Speak of salvation (He’ll later contrast salvation vs liberation).
  • Also:
    • Some say: “Do nothing; just witness.”
    • Others: “Daily prayer & rituals are must.”
    • Some say: “Supreme bhakti is goal.”
    • Others: “Self-realisation is goal.”
    • Others: “Merge in Oneness,” etc.

So the seeker:

“Gets lost in a maze of confusing ideologies, cobwebbed in many concepts.”

He neither knows:

  • What to do,
  • Nor how to proceed,
  • Nor where he is heading,
  • Nor where he should be heading.

He jokes:

  • Someone reads a Zen book:
    • “Do nothing, only witness,”
    • Immediately drops all his:
      • prayer,
      • seva,
      • bhakti, etc.
  • Then a saint scolds him:
    • “You’re doing nothing; how will you progress?”
    • Now he’s more confused.

He says:

“If we could pause and listen to a Jñāni (Enlightened One), we would know exactly:

  • where we are headed, and
  • where we should be headed.”

He deliberately uses Jñāni here, not yet “Sadguru”, because the verse uses jñāni:

  • Even if you haven’t accepted him as your Sadguru yet,
  • At least ensure:

“You listen to an Enlightened One,

not just an orator, pundit, or clever logician.”

8. Jail analogy – 4 gatis are 4 prisons

He gives a striking analogy:

  • Four gatis (forms of existence: heavenly, human, animal, hellish) = four jails:
    • Some jails:
      • Big grounds,
      • Good library,
      • Quite pleasant – like heaven.
    • Some:
      • Horrible smell,
      • Overcrowded,
      • Dark, filthy – like hell.
    • Some:
      • Ordinary – like human or animal realms.

He describes visiting various jails:

  • Tihar, Arthur Road, Yerwada, Byculla, Nagpur, Nasik, Thane…
  • Some so decent you might think:

“This is better than my house!”

  • Some (like Arthur Road):
    • He literally cried seeing the condition.

He shares:

  • Morari Bapu once stayed in Pune’s Yerwada jail (not as prisoner):
    • Lived like a prisoner for 9 days:
      • Same food,
      • Same cell,
      • Only went out to give kathā then returned.

His point:

“All four gatis are jails. Some jails are comfortable (deva), some miserable (narak), but they are all bondage. You need liberation from all four.”

So:

  • With superficial ritual, meditation, bhakti:
    • At most → 1st heaven, then back to jail.
  • Liberation (moksh) is total freedom from all gatis.

Thus:

“Once you listen to a Jñāni, you realise whether you’re only polishing your jail cell or actually working towards freedom.”

9. Purpose of explaining shatsthanak & shatdarshan

He distils Param Krupalu Dev’s noble aim:

Not to establish “my opinion” or negate others’ opinions, but to:

  • give a clear resolve & conviction about the truth,
  • show:
    • goal (moksh),
    • path (mokshmarg),
    • steps on the path.

He says:

  • If you deeply reflect on the dialogue:
    • You’ll recognise:
      • Different viewpoints appear,
      • Representing different philosophies,
      • But always resolved by integrating them into truth.

Finally, he summarises:

“Param Krupalu Dev’s aim is to draw your attention to the Self, not just to expose you to how philosophies differ and leave you more confused.”

That’s why:

  • It’s a Guru–disciple debate, full of:
    • Love,
    • Respect,
    • And razor-sharp reasoning.

10. Last condensed takeaway of Verse 44

Verse 44, in his spirit:

  • I (as Jñānī) will describe the six fundamentals briefly (śatsthanak saṅkṣepmā).
  • In doing so, I will also indirectly bring in the six major philosophies (śatdarshan pan teh).
  • Enlightened Ones have taught these, not for intellectual games, but to make you understand the supreme truth (samjāva parmarathne), so you know:
    • what is real,
    • what your goal is,
    • what path to walk,
    • and how to walk it.

From here, the stage is set.

  • Next:
    • The disciple will start questioning the first fundamental:

“Does the soul exist?”

  • Sadguru will answer with:
    • Gentle persuasion,
    • Logical clarity,
    • And the love of a Jñāni who wants your full freedom.
