• In the previous section (verses 24–33), Param Krupalu Dev:
    • Described the characteristics of a matārthī:
      • Sectarian, bigoted, either:
        • Mechanical ritualist (kriyājada matārthī), or
        • Dry intellectualist (śuṣk-jñānī matārthī).
    • Purpose: to help us eradicate bigotry by clearly seeing it within ourselves and giving it up.
  • That section ends with:
    • “Atma-arth sukh-sāj” – the true bliss of the soul;
      the pointer that the real goal is:
      • Well-being of the soul (spiritual welfare) and
      • Unbroken, unobstructed inner bliss.
  • Now, in this third section of the Shastra, Param Krupalu Dev:
    • Turns from “this is what a bigot looks like”
    • To “this is what a true seeker of the soul looks like.”

2. Who is an Ātmārthī?

  • Param Krupalu Dev uses the word ātmārthī for:

“True seeker of the soul whose only objective is the bliss and welfare of the soul.”

  • Meaning of ātmārthī:
    • Atma = soul
    • Artha = purpose / welfare / meaning
    • So ātmārthī = one whose life is centered on spiritual welfare and inner bliss.
  • Key points:
    • When one cultivates the virtues of an ātmārthī, one becomes:
      • Eligible for unbroken, unobstructed bliss.
    • For such a person:
      • The only aim in life is Self-realisation.
        • “Only” is very important:
        • They still perform roles – job, family, responsibilities.
        • But in the background of all of that, one single inner aim:

To realise the Self and experience the bliss of the soul by creating deep stillness within.

3. Why a whole positive section on the true seeker?

Param Krupalu Dev could have just said:

“One who is not a matārthī is an ātmārthī.”

or

“Whoever doesn’t behave like a bigot – that is a true seeker.”

That would’ve been the negative definition: “Nemiji is not like Maulikji; so you know what Nemiji must be like.”

But He doesn’t stop there. Instead:

  • He composes a full section (verses 34–42) describing:
    • The correct mindset
    • The convictions, inner clarity, and aim of an ātmārthī.

Why positive description?

He explains the subtle difference:

  • “Today, it is not hot” – negative description.
  • “Today, it is cool and pleasant” – positive description.

Both are true, but:

  • A positive description:

1. Inspires us.

2. Helps us cultivate and grow the virtues:

  • If they’re present, we strengthen them.
  • If they’re absent, we start cultivating them.

So Param Krupalu Dev chooses to:

  • Describe the ātmārthī directly and positively,
  • Not just define him as the “opposite of the bigot.”

4. Clarity before meeting the Sadguru

A really important insight:

  • These verses describe the ātmārthī’s mindset before he comes to a Sadguru.

Questions like:

  • What does he believe about life?
  • What is he seeking?
  • Why is he going to a Guru?
  • What does he want to receive from that association?

Because if you don’t have that clarity:

  • You may:
    • Come to the Guru for comfort, for scenery, for family pressure.
    • Behave with hidden irreverence or disrespect, simply because you never decided clearly why you came.

This gets summarised in one powerful phrase from later verses:

“Bijo nahi mann-rog” – No other mental disease of cravings.

Meaning:

  • An ātmārthī has no other craving in the mind:
    • Not for comfort, status, social circle, emotional security, etc.
    • Not for staying in some beautiful āshram as a “nice lifestyle.”
  • He is not a parasite on the sangh (community) – using spiritual surroundings to fulfil worldly desires.

Without this clarity, his entire pursuit can become futile.

5. The birth of a true seeker: from wandering to reflection

Param Krupalu Dev traces the inner journey of the one who becomes an ātmārthī:

1. Endless aimless wandering

  • The soul has been:
    • Wandering through birth and death since time immemorial – across all four gatis (realms), including deva gati (heavenly realms).
  • Even in celestial life:
    • They have:
      • Long life-spans,
      • Instant gratification (desire pizza → immediate burp, no cooking, chewing, digesting).
    • Yet:
      • After instantaneous fulfilment, there is: “Now what?”
      • Emptiness, boredom, no direction.
  • So even when all desires are easily fulfilled, the soul still does not turn within.

This shows:

Fulfilment of desires does not automatically awaken spirituality.

2. Beginning of reflection

A true seeker starts to reflect:

  • “Why have I been wandering like this from birth to birth?”
  • “Even when my desires were fulfilled, why didn’t I turn within?”
  • “Who am I, really? Where did I come from? What is happening and why?”

From such reflection arise basic questions, which later manifest as:

  • The six fundamental truths of Atmasiddhi (soul exists, is eternal, is doer, enjoyer, etc.).

But at this stage:

  • The ātmārthī:
    • Does not yet have all the answers.
    • But he starts questioning deeply.

3. Deep calling / painful yearning

Because his questions aren’t fully resolved:

  • His meditation and devotion acquire a different tone:
    • There is:
      • A deep calling, or
      • A painful yearning within.
  • He meditates, does bhakti daily, but:
    • He feels, “I’m still not hitting the target.”
    • This gap between:
      • What he longs for, and
      • What he actually experiences creates a sweet pain.

This is often experienced as:

  • Tears in devotion,
  • A sense of divine discontent,
  • Feeling: “Something higher is calling me, and I’m not there yet.”

He still lives the same outer life, but inside:

  • A restless longing has awakened.

4. Fading interest in worldly activities

As that deep calling strengthens:

  • He begins to notice:

1. Lack of interest in worldly activities:

  • He continues his duties—job, family, social events—but:
    • The old excitement is fading.
    • The curiosity and thrill of these activities begins to wane.
  • Sometimes:
    • He goes shopping or to some outing,
    • And afterwards, feels:

“What did I just do? Wasted time, money, and moved away from that inner connection.”

2. Reduction in urge for sensual pleasures:

  • His need for strong sensory experiences starts lowering.
  • Not because of forced suppression, but because:
    • He sees they really give him nothing substantial.

At first, he might say:

  • “The world gives only temporary happiness.”

Eventually he starts feeling:

  • “The world actually gives me no real happiness at all. Just noise and distraction.”

This is important:

  • These changes feel natural and experiential, not imposed by rules.

6. Questioning previous religious understanding

At this stage, the seeker looks back and realises:

  • Up till now, his understanding of:
    • God
    • Guru
    • Religion
    • Scriptures
    • Rituals
    • Clothing/attire came largely from:
    • Sectarian gurus,
    • Parents,
    • Paathshala (religious school),
    • Books interpreted in a limited sectarian way.

Now, because he wants experience, not just memorised concepts:

  • He begins to question everything he has adopted:
    • “Why am I doing this fast?”
    • “Why am I singing this bhajan – for music, or for inner transformation?”
    • “Is this the real reason to stay near a Guru or in this āshram?”
    • “Am I showing a holy image to the world while nothing moves within?”

Important nuance:

  • He is questioning, not necessarily rejecting.
  • Not everything from the past is wrong, but:
    • His relationship to it must shift from:
      • Blind acceptance → examined, experiential understanding.

7. This questioning reveals the need for a Sadguru

He questions, but:

  • He still doesn’t fully know:
    • Whether his answers are correct, or
    • Whether his introspections are complete.

This creates the next step:

A deep sense of: “I need a true Guru.”

  • Not just “any guru,” not just “family guru,” not just “sect guru”:
    • But a Sadguru—a true Enlightened Master.

And that’s why:

  • The very first verse of “ātmārthī na lakṣaṇ” (traits of the true seeker) begins by describing:

What kind of Guru an ātmārthī wants.

  • At this point:
    • He may not yet have found that Sadguru,
    • But he has clarity about:
      • What kind of Guru he seeks,
      • What qualities such a Guru must have.

This stands in contrast to the bigot in verse 24:

  • The matārthī’s idea of Guru:
    • Outwardly renounced, but not Self-realised,
    • Chosen based on family tradition or sect loyalty,
    • Attachment to “our guru” rather than truth.

The ātmārthī:

  • Wants a true Guru, not just a family/sect guru.

8. Overview of the coming verses (34–42)

The teacher then gives a map of the upcoming section, so you see the full journey of an ātmārthī:

Verses 34–36: Relationship with the Guru

1. Verse 34 – Concept of a true Guru:

  • What an ātmārthī thinks a right Guru should be like.

2. Verse 35 – Importance of a living Guru (pratyakṣ):

  • A living, blood-and-flesh Master whose presence guides.

3. Verse 36 – Obedience to the living true Guru:

  • Whatever command He gives:
    • Even if it’s “get married” or “do this worldly task,”
    • It is accepted as a step towards liberation.
  • The ātmārthī decides beforehand:
    • “Whatever He instructs, I will obey— because His every instruction leads me closer to the eternal path.”

Verse 37: Purpose of associating with the Guru

4. Verse 37 – Purpose of Guru’s association:

  • An ātmārthī comes to the Guru with:
    • Only one desire: Self-realisation.
    • “Bijo nahi mann-rog” – no other cravings:
      • Not for comfort, company, status, healing, miracles, etc.
  • If you come with mixed motives, you dilute your own journey.

Verses 38–41: Entire progress of the true seeker

5. Verses 38–41 – The full progress of an ātmārthī:

  • From starting point to final liberation:

1. Subsidence of passions and core attributes begin to arise.

2. Mind becomes receptive to the Guru’s teachings.

3. From that, right thinking / samyag-darshan–jñān arises.

4. Then Self-realisation occurs.

5. After that, delusion is destroyed, leading to Nirvāṇa / final liberation.

So these verses quietly hold:

The entire path from first aspiration to ultimate freedom.

Verse 42: Bridge to the next section

6. Verse 42 – A preparation verse:

  • Smoothly links:
    • The “true seeker” section,
    • To the main subject of Atmasiddhi – the six fundamental truths and direct Self-knowledge.

9. Essence of this introduction

In this talk, the teacher is basically doing three things:

1. Closing the “bigot” chapter in our heart:

  • Bigotry is not just about others;
  • It is about the subtle ways we misuse religion for ego.

2. Opening the doorway to becoming an ātmārthī:

  • Showing:
    • How a true seeker is born from:
      • Wandering → reflection → deep calling → fading worldly interest → questioning → need for a Sadguru.
  • And making it feel:
    • More experiential than philosophical – like something you are already in the midst of.

3. Giving a roadmap of the next 9 verses:

  • So as you listen/study, you can say:
    • “Oh, this is about Guru.”
    • “This is about obedience.”
    • “This is about my only aim.”
    • “This is about the inner progression to liberation.”

One-line wrap-up

This video is the doorway from “I now see the bigot within me” to “I now want to become an ātmārthī” – someone whose only aim is Self-realisation, who feels a deep inner calling, and who now seeks, with full clarity, the guidance of a true living Guru and the path to the bliss of the soul.
