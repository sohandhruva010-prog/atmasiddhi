• Verses 24–32 (9 verses total): Param Krupalu Dev has just finished describing, in detail:
    • The unfortunate one – durbhāgī
    • The unworthy one – an-adhikārī
    • The matārthī – sectarian / bigoted:
      • Kriyājada matārthī – mechanical ritualist
      • Śuṣk-jñānī matārthī – dry intellectualist
  • These 9 verses:
    • Expose how such a person is religiously active but spiritually off-track.
    • Are full of strong words (fool, bigot, etc.), yet all used out of compassion.
  • Verse 33 (this discourse) is:
    • Conclusion of the “bigot” section, not the whole Shastra.
    • A bridge between:
      • The description of the matārthī (bigot), and
      • The upcoming description of the ātmārthī (true seeker).

2. The verse in simple breakdown

The verse has two main parts:

A. First line – Purpose of describing the bigot

Lakshan kahyā matārthīnā – matārth java kāj
  • “I have described the characteristics of a bigot…”
  • Why? → “…for the purpose of removing bigotry.”

Key message:

  • The point was not:
    • To mock, shame, or condemn.
  • The point was:
    • So that you and I can:
      • Recognise bigotry within, and
      • Remove it from our heart.

Importantly:

  • He does not say: "Stop all rituals / vows / austerities."
  • He says: “Remove the bigoted attitude, not the activities themselves.”
    • The activities (fasts, bhakti, charity) can be excellent,
    • But egoic, sectarian style of doing them is the problem.

B. Second line – Pledge to describe the true seeker

Havey kahu ātmārthīnā – ātmārtha sukh-sāj
  • “Now I will describe the characteristics of an ātmārthī (true seeker)…”
  • Why? → “…for the purpose of ātmārtha sukh-sāj” – to help you manifest the bliss of the soul.

Clarifications:

  • He will describe:
    • Not what activities a true seeker does (how many fasts, etc.),
    • But the inner state and virtues of a true seeker.
  • A true seeker’s sole purpose:
    • Inner bliss,
    • Self-realisation,
    • Manifesting the inherent joy of the soul.

So this verse says:

1. “I showed you the bigot so you can remove bigotry.”

2. “Now I will show you the true seeker so you can cultivate those traits and attain inner bliss.”

3. Why talk so much about a bigot in a book called Atmasiddhi?

You might wonder:

“This Shastra is called Atmasiddhi (attainment of the Self). Why not just directly describe the true seeker and how he attains the Self? Why spend 9 verses on a foolish bigot?”

Answer: this is the heart of the verse.

Key insight:

“There is no greater saint than a reformed sinner.”
  • Every saint has a past,
  • Every sinner has a future.

Applied here:

  • “Bigot” doesn’t mean hopeless.
  • In Param Krupalu Dev’s heart it is very clear:
    • If a bigot:
      • Clearly sees his mistakes,
      • Feels their harm,
      • And removes them,
    • He can become a great saint.

So:

  • He explains the matārthī in detail, not to label someone for life, but:
    • So the person can see himself clearly,
    • Separate what is truly spiritual from what only looks spiritual,
    • And transform.

4. “Marking the territory” – what is NOT spirituality

The teacher gives a metaphor:

  • In Africa, hippos:
    • Are pure vegetarians, but
    • Extremely territorial – they mark their area, and if others enter, they can even kill large animals like giraffes or crocodiles.

The point is not to mimic the aggression, but to learn the clarity of territory:

  • A reformed bigot, through deep introspection, can say:
    • “This zone of behaviour, thinking, ego and pride is definitely not spirituality.”
    • “And this other zone – humility, detachment, simplicity – is rightly spirituality.”

He learns from experience:

  • “Without simplicity, my spirituality is ruined.”
  • “Without impartiality, I become stubborn and egoistic.”
  • “Without inner detachment, my mind constantly wanders in worldly desires while everyone respects me – I’m tired of this double life.”

Once he clearly marks:

  • “This is to be avoided; this is to be embraced,”
  • Then he doesn’t have to hunt for Self-realisation everywhere.
  • If he avoids these pitfalls sincerely, the path opens naturally.

5. The most powerful word: “Locate”

The teacher highlights one key word for this session:

Locate.

Once you locate your inner mistake, the game changes.

He breaks down four levels where mistakes can lie:

1. Behaviour – outward actions.

2. Speech – choice of words.

3. Feelings – what you actually feel inside.

4. Belief – what you hold as true in your understanding.

Example – speech mistake

  • Saying to a girl: “You’re looking fat.”
  • He jokes: you never use “fat” for a girl. The “heaviest” acceptable word is “healthy.”
  • Here:
    • Intention may not be bad,
    • But it’s still a mistake – at the level of words.
  • So he locates:
    • “The problem was not in my belief or feeling, but in my expression.”

Body-pain examples – instinctive self-protection

  • If one small area of your jaw is hurting:
    • Even if someone goes to touch that spot, you naturally protect it.
  • Similarly, if someone has a bone pain in the arm:
    • He naturally holds or moves in a way that guards the painful part.

From this, he says:

Once you locate where your mistake is, your entire being automatically starts protecting and correcting itself.

  • You don’t even need constant external guidance:
    • Just like nobody has to tell you how to avoid pressing on your painful jaw.
  • The earlier problem was:
    • We were not even locating our mistakes.
    • That’s why we have been wandering in transmigration since beginningless time.

So:

  • Step 1: Realise the danger of your tendencies (anger, ego, vanity, sectarianism).
  • Step 2: Locate the exact level:
    • Is my mistake in my belief?
    • In my feeling?
    • In my behaviour?
    • Or only in my words?

Once located:

  • The mind starts purifying,
  • Becomes more focused and receptive.

And then:

  • Guru’s mere presence – not just His words – begins to uplift you.

6. Stop diagnosing others: the “deaf kaka” story

We naturally hear these bigot descriptions and think:

  • “That person is definitely a dry intellectual bigot.”
  • “That lady is surely a mechanical ritualist, always fasting…”

To break this tendency, the teacher narrates a humorous story:

The story

  • A kaka (elderly man) goes to a doctor and says:

1. “My wife (kaki) is getting deaf. I think she needs a hearing aid.”

  • Doctor says:

1. “I need to know from what distance she stops hearing. Check and tell me.”

  • Kaka goes home and tests:

1. From the door, he shouts: “What vegetable have you made today?” → No reply.

2. Comes to the kitchen entrance: same question. → No reply.

3. Comes closer: same question. → Still no reply.

4. Stands just behind kaki, asks again:

  • She snaps: “I’ve told you four times – eggplant curry! How many times should I say?!”

Punchline:

  • Who was actually deaf? → The kaka, not the kaki.

Moral:

  • We keep feeling others are wrong, deaf, bigots, sectarian.
  • But often, the fault lies in us.
  • Param Krupalu Dev’s descriptions are not to label others:

They are mirrors to help you identify your own bigotry.

7. The style of compassion: making wrong states feel painful

Param Krupalu Dev:

  • Doesn’t just say:
    • “Don’t be a bigot.”
    • “Don’t be sectarian.”
  • Instead, He describes:
    • The subtle tendencies,
    • The hidden motives (like wanting praise for fasting),
    • The inner objective behind the same outer act.

This is done so vividly that:

  • You can’t escape by hiding behind, “I just did it as a ritual” or “I didn’t mean it.”
  • You see:
    • “On day 5 of my fast, I did want to stop but continued only so that people would praise my willpower.”
    • So, yes – ego was the real objective.

This is compassion in action:

  • He doesn’t just make you aware of your wrong state,
  • He makes that state feel painful and ugly inside:
    • It stops being only “technically wrong,”
    • It starts feeling intolerable to you.

Difference:

  • Mere awareness: “Yes, I am angry.”
  • Deeper impact: “I can’t bear this state; I hate being in this anger/ego zone.”

Only an Enlightened Master can do this so precisely:

  • Turn your attachment to ego into disgust for ego.
  • That is what truly helps you remove bigotry.

8. Fulfilment of His earlier pledge: nirpakṣa (impartiality)

In verse 23, Param Krupalu Dev had pledged:

“I will speak impartially (nirpakṣa).”

  • Not influenced by:
    • Sect (Śvetāmbara, Digambara, etc.),
    • Caste, age, gender, region, or group.

In this section on the bigot:

  • He has spoken:
    • Bluntly, but
    • Fairly,
    • With equally sharp clarity for both:
      • Mechanical ritualists, and
      • Dry intellectuals.

So in verse 33, we can feel:

  • That pledge has been honoured completely.
  • We feel:
    • “All these traits are in me.”
    • “And by His grace, all of them can also go from me.”

9. Two key takeaways from this verse

The teacher says you learned two special words today:

1. Locate

  • Once you locate your mistake, your whole being starts to protect and correct itself.

2. Uplift

  • Everything the Enlightened Master does – His speaking, His silence, His strictness, His sweetness – is only to uplift you, never to put you down.

If you’re not deeply trapped in bigotry:

  • You will reflect on these 9 verses.
  • You will see yourself in them.
  • You will want to run away from those tendencies.

10. Transition: from bigot to true seeker (Ātmārthī)

Verse 33 marks the end of the matārthī section:

  • “Characteristics of the bigot have been described for the purpose of removing bigotry.”

Now:

  • Param Krupalu Dev says:

“Havey kahu ātmārthīnā…” “Now I will describe the characteristics of a true seeker.”

  • From verse 34 to 42 (9 verses):
    • He will present the traits of an ātmārthī,
    • One less verse than for the bigot (delicately noted).
Purpose of the upcoming section:
  • To turn your attention:
    • From outer activities to the soul,
    • From borrowed joy to inherent bliss.
  • To show how the bliss already within you can be:
    • Recognised,
    • Manifested,
    • Abided in.

He will begin that from the next verse:

  • Leading you from:
    • “This is what to avoid”
    • To “This is what to become.”

One-sentence wrap-up

After nine verses of exposing the matārthī, verse 33 gently reveals the real purpose: not to condemn you, but to help you locate and remove your bigotry, and then, with the same compassion, to guide you into the inner qualities of a true seeker, so that the bliss of the soul can finally shine forth.
