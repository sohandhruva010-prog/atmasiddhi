The big aim: Self-realisation (Atmasiddhi)

  • He opens by restating the overarching project: understanding the Six Fundamental Truths “regarding the soul” for Self-realisation.
  • He clarifies wording: not “truths of the soul” but “truths regarding the soul” (because the last two truths extend beyond just “what the soul is,” into outcomes like bondage/liberation).

Quick recap of the first three Fundamental Truths

1. Soul exists (“atma chhe”)

2. Soul is eternal (“atma nitya chhe”)

3. Soul is the doer — specifically:

  • doer of actions (karma as action) and/or
  • doer of karma-bandh (karma as bondage / acquiring karmic matter)

He repeats that whichever meaning of karma you take, the Third Truth is:

the soul is the “karta” (doer) of it.

2) The disciple’s core doubt (the engine of these verses)

What the disciple doubts

  • The disciple doubts the Third Truth:
    • “The soul is the doer of karmas” → he doubts this
  • The disciple leans toward the opposite:
    • the soul is non-doer (non-agent)
    • even though it “appears” to act

So the disciple’s position is:

  • It looks like the soul acts
  • But in reality the soul is not the doer (not doer of actions; not doer of bondage)

Why the doubt creates a bigger problem

He points out a pressure point:

  • If the soul is not the doer… then why does the soul:
    • receive results?
    • suffer/endure?
    • enjoy?
    • experience joy/sorrow and diversity?

This connects directly to the Fourth Fundamental Truth (enjoyer/experiencer of fruits). So the disciple is stuck:

  • he isn’t fully clear on doership,
  • and he isn’t even fully sure the “non-doer” idea can explain suffering.

Small but important teaching move here

He’s showing: the disciple’s doubt is not casual. It creates consequences for the whole system:

  • doership
  • responsibility
  • fruits
  • bondage
  • liberation

3) Recap of Verse 71 (yesterday’s three alternatives)

He quickly summarizes Verse 71 (which you studied in the prior video):

Verse 71’s three alternatives (all deny soul’s responsibility)

1. New karmas arise from past karmas

  • chain of actions/reactions
  • karmas “do” karmas
  • soul becomes non-doer

2. Automatic / accidental

  • karmas bind without a specific reason/condition
  • “it just happens”
  • again soul isn’t responsible

3. It’s the soul’s innate nature

  • soul does/acquires karmas because that’s what it is
  • therefore it wasn’t “choice,” so soul still isn’t morally responsible
Common conclusion across all three:

the soul is not responsible.

The cook/enjoyer analogy (introduced here)

He anticipates the classic objection:

  • “If the soul didn’t do it, why does it enjoy/suffer the fruits?”

He gives a simple model of “one does, another enjoys”:

  • Cook makes food
  • You enjoy food
  • so “doer” and “enjoyer” can be different

He flags that the Guru will resolve this properly later.

4) What Verse 72 adds (today’s two additional alternatives)

Where we are in the verse structure

  • Verse 71 gave 3 alternatives
  • Verse 72 will give 2 more alternatives
  • The next verse will contain the disciple’s conclusion from all of them

So Verse 72 = alternatives #4 and #5 in the disciple’s “five-option” set.

Key claim: Verse 72’s arguments come from Vedic philosophies

He says plainly:

  • Verse 72 is strongly influenced by Vedic philosophy, especially:
    • Sankhya (for the “prakriti” argument)
    • Nyaya (for the “Ishwar-prerna” argument)

Why he says “clearly visible”

Because the verse uses signature vocabulary:

  • prakriti → a Sankhya term
  • Ishwar-prerna (God as inspirer/prompter) → a Nyaya framing

He notes:

  • Param Krupalu Dev does not name these schools in the text.

5) Big “background map”: the philosophy landscape he outlines

The “pairs” model

He lists the “Vedic six” as:

  • Nyaya
  • Vaisheshika
  • Sankhya
  • Yoga
  • Purva Mimansa
  • Uttara Mimansa (Vedanta)

Then he also describes the broader set often counted as:

  • Charvaka
  • Jainism
  • Buddhism
  • Nyaya + Vaisheshika (pair)
  • Sankhya + Yoga (pair)
  • Purva + Uttara Mimansa (pair)

What the last three pairs have in common

They accept the authority of the Vedas and develop interpretations from them.

He uses a strong image:

  • One set of Vedas → multiple interpretations → fully separate established philosophies.

6) A spiritual/psychological “how to be a disciple” section (very important in this talk)

This isn’t side chatter; it’s a lesson about how confusion should be handled.

Whirlpool of doubts + eagerness = the mark of a true seeker

He says:

  • You can be confused (whirlpool of doubts)
  • but a true seeker doesn’t conclude:
    • “this is rubbish,” “too confusing,” “I’m out”

Instead:

  • the true seeker holds faith that:
    • a Sadguru can resolve it
    • the guru will show where you grabbed a partial truth and treated it as the full truth

Two kinds of faith

He distinguishes:

1. faith in the Guru

2. faith in the metaphysical truth-system

The disciple is unclear about the metaphysics but clear in faith toward the Guru—so he doesn’t quit.

The importance of communication

He describes the process:

  • contemplate repeatedly
  • but if you’re stuck in confusion (or making wrong judgments), you need communication with the guru to correct it

He references the disciple’s request style (“samjavo sadupay”):

  • asking for a satisfying, correct solution
  • with humility: “my reasoning may be wrong, my observation may be incomplete”

7) The “shoes” volunteer-worker story (what it illustrates)

This long example is used to explain “asang” (non-attachment) and also the difference between literalism vs essence.

The story

  • Instruction to visitors: “remove your shoes”
  • A dense volunteer worker, hearing someone say “I’m not wearing shoes,” responds:
    • “Then wear shoes, then remove them, then enter”
  • The teacher uses it to show:
    • sticking to words without understanding the meaning is foolish

His deeper definition of “swayamsevak” (volunteer)

He gives two layers:

1. basic meaning: someone voluntarily offers seva (service)

2. his preferred meaning: someone who:

  • understands the essence of the guru’s instruction
  • can adapt as conditions change while keeping the essence intact (instead of rigid literalism)

“TEAM has no I”

He says:

  • a real “team” is not just 4–5 people together
  • it’s a team when there is no ‘I’ (ego)
  • when ego enters, it becomes:
    • clashes
    • power games
    • spirituality gets lost

Why he says all this here: Because verse 72 is about “soul is always unattached / unbound.” He’s warming you up to understand what “asang” really implies and how people misunderstand words.

8) Verse 72: the two arguments (the actual verse content in plain meaning)

He frames Verse 72 as: The disciple offers two more ways to show the soul is non-doer.

Argument #4 (Sankhya influence)

“Atma sada asang… karey prakruti bandh”

Meaning:

  • The soul is always unattached (asang), always
  • Bondage is done by prakriti (matter/nature)

So:

  • soul is not the doer of bondage
  • prakriti is the doer of bondage

Argument #5 (Nyaya influence)

“Athva Ishwar prerna… tethi jiva abandh”

Meaning:

  • Or: everything happens by God’s inspiration/prompting
  • therefore the soul remains unbound (abandh) and non-doer

So:

  • soul appears active
  • but “real doer” is God
  • soul is not responsible

9) Deep dive: Sankhya argument (prakriti does bondage)

He spends a LOT of time here because most listeners “don’t know Sankhya,” and without it the line “prakriti binds” feels vague.

9.1 What “Sankhya” means (as he explains)

He breaks it as “San-khya”:

  • San = good/right/true
  • khya = to tell/declare So Sankhya is:
  • a philosophy that “tells correctly with discrimination”
  • its hallmark is discrimination between:
    • Purush (consciousness/soul)
    • Prakriti (non-conscious matter)

He parallels Jain terms:

  • Purush vs prakriti ≈ chetan vs jada

9.2 His personal story (why he can talk on Sankhya)

He recounts:

  • 1989: moving university; had to take an entrance test to do an MA in philosophy
  • he studied while traveling due to his father’s surgery abroad
  • exam had Indian and Western philosophy papers
  • he got Sankhya for Indian philosophy (and Spinoza for Western)
  • he says he could talk about Sankhya for “10 days,” so compressing it is hard

(The function of this story: to justify why he’s giving a simplified intro and why details are being withheld.)

9.3 Characteristics of Sankhya (as he defines it)

  • Realistic (focuses on what “is,” not just what “appears”)
  • Dualistic (two primary categories)
    • unlike Charvaka (matter-only)
    • unlike Vedanta (spirit-only)
  • Pluralistic about souls
    • not “one soul only” like some Advaita positions
    • “prati dehe”: each body has a soul (many souls)

9.4 Propounder + historical notes (as he mentions)

  • Propounder: Maharshi Kapila
  • historians: Sankhya prevalent before Buddha (he cites “hundred years before Buddha” as what historians claim)
  • says the word appears in:
    • Samaysaar
    • Bhagavad Gita (“Sankhya Yog” / Sankhya discussions)
  • he also mentions a tradition he has read: Kapila as a past-life link to Gautamswami (as a story he recalls reading)

(He treats this more as “things I’ve read / general knowledge,” not as the core proof.)

9.5 The heart of Sankhya: two categories

Sankhya divides everything into:

1. Purush = soul / consciousness

2. Prakriti = matter / nature (non-conscious) This directly matches the verse:

  • Purush (atma) is always asang (unattached)
  • prakriti produces bondage

9.6 The 25 “elements” model (tattvas)

He states Sankhya’s structure:

  • Total 25 elements
    • 1 is Purush (category-wise one, though many individual souls)
    • 24 are prakriti’s evolutes/forms

Purush (soul) qualities in Sankhya (as he lists)

Purush is described as:

  • always pure
  • unchanging (no modifications)
  • eternal
  • unattached, unbound
  • inactive (non-doer)
  • not body, not mind, not intellect, not ego
    • all these belong to prakriti

He emphasizes:

  • if Purush “does,” it becomes doer → Sankhya refuses that
  • Purush is portrayed as a passive witness/spectator

Prakriti qualities (two key terms)

Prakriti is:

  • jadatmika = non-conscious
  • trigunatmika = composed of three gunas

The three gunas:

1. sattva (goodness/clarity)

2. rajas (activity)

3. tamas (inertia)

9.7 The braid example (three strands = three gunas)

He asks someone with braided hair to open the braid:

  • the braid looks like “one”
  • but it has three strands This is his visual for:
  • prakriti looks unified
  • but is made of three qualities

Then he adds the Sankhya mechanism:

  • prakriti is “balanced” when gunas are in equilibrium
  • contact with Purush disturbs the balance
  • then prakriti “evolves” into its 24 elements

9.8 The 24 prakriti elements: the 5 groups he lists

He avoids technical Sanskrit names intentionally, but gives the grouped breakdown:

1. Five gross elements: earth, water, fire, air, space

2. Five sense organs (jnanendriya): the organs by which you perceive/know

3. Five action organs (karmendriya): speech, hands, feet, anus, genitals (organs through which action is performed)

4. Five sense objects: touch, smell, taste, sight, sound

5. Four inner instruments: mind, intellect, ego, tendency (he also mentions conscience may be counted here)

Why he explains this at all: Because the confusion in Sankhya hinges on where intellect belongs.

9.9 The key Sankhya trick: intellect is prakriti, but reflects consciousness

He highlights Sankhya’s explanation:

  • Intellect is a form of prakriti → so it is non-conscious
  • but consciousness “reflects” on it (purush reflected)
  • so intellect appears:
    • active (because it’s prakriti)
    • conscious (because purush reflects)

Sankhya’s conclusion from this

  • The “doer and enjoyer” is not purush (soul)
  • It is the intellect (prakriti category) that appears to do/enjoy
  • Therefore:
    • bondage happens to prakriti
    • liberation happens to prakriti
    • purush was never bound, never liberated

He pushes the listener with questions:

  • who gets bound: purush or prakriti?
  • in Sankhya’s model: prakriti (because intellect is prakriti)

9.10 The handkerchief/rope illustration (Sankhya’s “it’s tied to itself” logic)

He uses a dramatic demo:

  • ties a handkerchief around someone’s hand
  • then says, in the Sankhya “ultimate” view:
    • “the handkerchief is tied to the handkerchief”

Meaning:

  • from practical viewpoint: hand looks bound
  • from “reality” viewpoint: bondage is within prakriti itself

He connects it to the cow/rope image:

  • practically: cow is tied by rope
  • “ultimate”: rope is tied to rope

He names the two viewpoints:

  • Vyavharik = practical viewpoint
  • Parmarthik = real/absolute viewpoint

9.11 The crystal example (appearing vs being)

He uses a crystal metaphor:

  • crystal is colorless
  • place a blue object near it → it appears blue
  • remove the blue object → crystal appears clear again
  • but crystal itself never changed

Sankhya parallel:

  • soul (purush) appears bound when near prakriti activity
  • but purush remains unchanged/pure
  • bondage is “appearance,” not soul’s actual state

He briefly has someone recite a verse expressing:

  • “I am pure consciousness/bliss, unchanged, neither captive nor free” (used to reinforce the Sankhya style claim: always free in reality)

9.12 The “king wins the war” example (credit mismatch)

He gives a social analogy:

  • soldiers fight
  • commander fights
  • but people say: “the king won / the king lost”
  • meanwhile the king was eating comfortably

Meaning:

  • prakriti “does”
  • but everything is credited to purush
  • purush gets blamed/credited though it didn’t act

He uses the word “nartaki” (dancer):

  • prakriti is the dancer doing the whole play
  • purush is just the one things are attributed to

9.13 What the disciple is trying to prove with Sankhya

From this viewpoint:

  • “Atma/purush is always asang (unattached)”
  • “Bondage is caused by prakriti” Therefore:
  • the Third Fundamental Truth (“soul is the doer”) looks wrong from this philosophy’s framing

Important meta-point he adds: He warns that this argument is a partial truth that feels like a full truth—so the Guru will later correct it.

10) Deep dive: Nyaya argument (God inspires actions)

Compared to Sankhya, he says this part is “easy,” but he still gives a detailed map.

10.1 Why this is Nyaya

He repeats:

  • “Ishwar-prerna” language is Nyaya-ish
  • followers are called Naiyayikas

10.2 Nyaya basics as he explains

  • Propounder: Maharshi Gautama
  • he calls Nyaya:
    • “logical realism”
  • he mentions:
    • later development “Navya Nyaya” (by Gangesh)
    • after Navya Nyaya, Nyaya and Vaisheshika merge closely
  • he adds historical color:
    • Nyaya was strong around Alexander’s time; Alexander studied it in India (Takshashila/Gandhar region)

(Again: the role here is to show this is an established philosophical system, not a random belief.)

10.3 Who believes in God as “creator” (his quick survey)

He clarifies he means:

  • God as creator of the universe (not “God” as “liberated soul” like Jain use)

He runs through the philosophies:

  • Charvaka: no
  • Jainism: no (God as perfected soul, not creator)
  • Buddhism: no
  • Nyaya/Vaisheshika: yes
  • Sankhya: no (only purush+prakriti)
  • Yoga: God as ideal/witness, not creator
  • Purva Mimansa: doesn’t accept creator God (ritual deities yes)
  • Vedanta: mixed; he points out Advaita’s “world as appearance” makes “creator” complicated
    • he says Madhva tradition strongly supports creator-God

Bottom line for this verse: Nyaya is a key “creator + prompter God” system.

10.4 What “Ishwar-prerna” contains (three claims he

stresses)

He says this is “important for the quiz,” and asks everyone to write it.

Nyaya-style belief presented here:

1. God is creator of the universe

2. God is creator of souls (or at least organizer of souls—he later nuances this)

3. God is the prompter/inspirer of all actions of souls

He then uses a memory gimmick:

  • G-O-D = Generator, Operator, Destroyer (Brahma, Vishnu, Mahesh) (not as strict doctrine proof, more as a mnemonic for divine cosmic control)

10.5 “Everything is by God’s will” (leaf movement example)

He says Nyaya-style theism can claim:

  • even the movement of a leaf is God’s will
  • thoughts you get are inspired
  • actions you perform are inspired
  • results are arranged

He contrasts this with a Jain sentiment:

  • Jainism says one doesn’t have power to create even two pieces of grass (the contrast is: Jainism rejects a creator God who micromanages the world)

10.6 Instrumental cause vs material cause (potter analogy)

He clarifies a more technical Nyaya detail:

  • God is like a potter:
    • the clay (matter) already exists
    • the potter shapes it
  • so God is instrumental cause, not material cause

He explicitly says:

  • matter already exists
  • souls may already exist
  • God “creates the universe” by organizing/operating on existing stuff

He tells the listener:

  • if the potter metaphor is too hard, just remember the basic: “Nyaya says God is creator.”

10.7 Nyaya Kusumanjali / “Ishwar Siddhi” (proving God exists)

He references a scripture:

  • Nyaya Kusumanjali by Udayan Acharya
  • devoted to proving God’s existence (“Ishwar Siddhi”)
  • he mentions types of arguments used:
    • ontological
    • teleological
    • moral
    • metaphysical
    • scriptural

He also mentions studying Nyaya for a long period and writing such arguments in his MA.

Why he brings this up: To show that “God as creator/prompter” is philosophically argued, not just asserted.

10.8 Potter vs sun/moon argument (and his critique)

He explains an intuitive Nyaya line:

  • if a pot has a maker (potter)
  • then sun and moon must also have a maker
  • therefore: God is the maker

Then he uses that to raise critical questions:

  • If God can make mountains/oceans, why doesn’t God build the ashram too?
  • If God keeps the sun working, why doesn’t God light our lamps directly?

This shows:

  • the system sounds beautiful
  • but it raises contradictions about human effort and responsibility

10.9 Emotional “God’s will” vs metaphysical “no soul effort”

He makes an important distinction:

  • Saying “God’s will” emotionally (gratitude, devotion) is one thing.
  • Saying “God’s will” metaphysically as a total denial of soul effort is another.

The verse’s argument is the second kind:

  • souls are puppets
  • no real agency
  • therefore soul is not the doer

10.10 Ghost-possession analogy (the strongest example in this section)

He asks you to visualize:

  • a person possessed by a spirit/ghost
  • the person behaves differently, speaks differently
  • later the person says: “I don’t remember / I didn’t do it” Then asks:
  • who is responsible—the person or the ghost?

He reinforces with extreme examples he claims people report:

  • possessed person drinks boiling milk without pain
  • after the spirit leaves, burns/blisters appear
His intended philosophical mapping:
  • replace “ghost” with “God”
  • if all action is prompted by God,
    • the soul is like the possessed person
    • God is like the possessing agent
    • therefore soul should not be held responsible

This is exactly how the disciple tries to prove:

  • soul is non-doer
  • God is the doer

11) What Verse 72 is concluding (disciple’s logic)

He restates the combined force of the two options:

If Sankhya option:

  • prakriti does bondage
  • soul remains always asang/abandh → non-doer

If Nyaya option:

  • God inspires everything
  • soul remains abandh → non-doer

So either way:

  • soul cannot be the doer
  • therefore the Third Fundamental Truth seems doubtful (from the disciple’s current reasoning)

12) The big consequence he highlights at the end

He ends by pushing the logic to its conclusion:

  • If the soul is not bound at all,
  • then asking “how will it be liberated?” is foolish, because liberation only makes sense if bondage is real.

So the disciple’s reasoning threatens the later truths:

  • if non-doership implies non-bondage,
  • then liberation becomes meaningless.

And that sets the stage:

  • Now we must see how the Guru answers.
