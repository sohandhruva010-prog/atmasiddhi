• We’ve finished the section on matārthī (bigot/sectarian) – verses 24–33.
    • There, Param Krupalu Dev exposed the wrong tendencies of a bigot so we can recognise and remove them.
  • Now begins the 3rd section: “Ātmārthī na Lakṣaṇ” – attributes of a true seeker.
  • Before talking about:
    • Fasts,
    • Austerities,
    • External practices,
  • He starts with the most fundamental point on the path:

What is the ātmārthī’s understanding of a true Guru – a Sadguru? Verse 34 is the first step.

2. The verse in essence: “Atmajnān tyā munipanu…”

Param Krupalu Dev’s main line:

“Atmajnān tyā munipanu – where there is Self-realisation, there is monkhood.” “Te sāchā Guru hoy – such a one is the true Guru.” “Bākī kulguru kalpanā – following family / traditional Guru without Self-realisation is imagination.” “Ātmārthī nahi joy – a true seeker does not see him as Sadguru.”

From this, the teacher draws:

  • True monkhood:
    • Not just white clothes or external renunciation,
    • But Self-realisation + abiding in the Self.
  • True Guru (Sadguru):
    • One who is Self-realised, whether monk or not.
  • Kulguru kalpanā:
    • Just thinking, “He is our family Guru, so he must be my Sadguru,” without Self-realisation, is called kalpanā – mental projection, delusion.
  • The ātmārthī knows this and refuses to be fooled by:
    • Clothes,
    • Lineage,
    • Austerities,
    • Or “He’s been our Guru for 4 generations.”

3. Why does the verse suddenly mention “munipanu”?

The teacher pauses on this:

We are talking about Sadguru, so why has Param Krupalu Dev suddenly used the word “munipanu” (monkhood)?

He explains using Jain cultural context:

In Jain culture:

  • Most of us are born Jain. So what happens naturally?
    • We see a sadhu/sādhvī walking → hands automatically fold.
    • We call them “Guruji,” “Gurumā,” “Guruni.”
  • The unspoken assumption:

Monk = Guru. Nun = Guru. “Of course they’re Self-realised.”

In other traditions:

  • In many non-Jain traditions:
    • Gurus are often householders:
      • Living with spouse and children,
      • In a normal home,
      • Yet recognised as Enlightened.
  • There the criterion is: Enlightenment / Self-realisation, not external renunciation.

Back to Jain mindset:

  • In Jain thinking:
    • There is often no place for a householder Guru.
    • So any monk/nun automatically becomes “Guruji” in people’s minds, whether Self-realised or not.
  • This verse comes to correct that default equation:

Outer monkhood ≠ automatically Sadguru. True monkhood = where there is Self-realisation.

That’s why munipanu appears: to break the blind link “monk → Guru” and replace it with “Self-realised → true monk → Sadguru”.

4. The first trait of ātmārthī: his inner understanding of a Sadguru

Important nuance:

Verse 34 is NOT yet about him meeting a Sadguru. It’s about what is already clear inside him before he meets one.

  • Primary attribute of an ātmārthī: He has a correct understanding of what a true Sadguru must be.
  • That understanding is:
    • The non-negotiable requirement = Self-realisation.
    • Renunciation, learning, oratory, austerities = all good, but not enough.

So he concludes:

“If I truly want Self-realisation, I must take refuge only in someone who has Self-realisation.”

5. Contrast with the matārthī (verse 24)

The teacher connects it back to the earlier section:

  • Matārthī (bigot) in verse 24:
    • Sees outer tyāg (renunciation) and immediately assumes: “He is a true Guru.”
    • Doesn’t check inner state at all.
  • Ātmārthī:
    • Values Self-realisation over robes.
    • Still:
      • Respects monks/nuns,
      • Serves them (sādharmik bhakti, swamivātsalya),
      • Honours their discipline and sacrifices.
    • BUT: He does not automatically equate them with Sadguru.

So the same person may get:

  • Respect as:
    • Renunciate,
    • Scholar,
  • But not the final surrender of Sadguru unless there is:
    • Conviction of Self-realisation.

That’s what shifts him from matārthī → ātmārthī.

6. “If you want notes, you’re a student. If you want experience, you’re an ātmārthī.”

Here he shifts to the inner hunger of the seeker:

  • A student:
    • Wants good notes, clear concepts, definitions.
  • An ātmārthī:
    • Wants direct experience of the Self – not just theory.

He shows why this longing arises:

  • We are all:
    • Busy in professions,
    • Family roles,
    • To-do lists,
    • Thoughts and identities.
  • In doing all this:
    • We lose touch with our true Self.
  • Worse:
    • To rediscover ourselves, we ask help from wrong people:
      • Family guru who himself doesn’t know the Self,
      • Someone with external renunciation but no Self-realisation.

He says:

One bad thing: I lost touch with my Self. Worse: I am taking help from wrong quarters.

That’s why the seeker’s search doesn’t bear fruit until he finds the right key.

7. Lock-and-key: why Sadguru comes before Self-realisation

Beautiful analogy he uses:

  • Self = a locked treasure.
  • Sadguru = the correct key that actually fits the lock.

If:

  • The key doesn’t fit:
    • The lock will never open,
    • Ignorance will not be removed.

So the order is:

1. First, find the right key → Sadguru.

2. Then, the process of Self-realisation becomes possible.

He says:

Right now, don’t jump directly to “I want Self-realisation.” First: “Who is the right Sadguru I should take refuge in?”

That’s what verse 34 is clarifying: the ātmārthī’s preparation and clarity before meeting Sadguru.

8. Revisiting Verse 10: 5 traits of a Sadguru – and what society ignores

He recalls verse 10 (Sadguru’s characteristics) and asks someone to list them:

1. Self-realisation (ātmajnān)

2. Equanimity

3. Lives as per past karmas, without egoic interference

4. Unique, awakening speech

5. Deep scriptural knowledge Then he comments:

  • Society rarely talks about Self-realisation as the first criterion.
  • Instead, people look for:
    • Personality,
    • Years of renunciation,
    • Scriptural scholarship,
    • Dashing presence,
    • Size of following.
  • Self-realisation becomes:

“If it’s there, very good; if not, no big deal.”

But the ātmārthī puts the list back in the right order:

For him, Self-realisation is first and essential. Everything else is secondary and optional.

9. Support from Āchārāṅg Sūtra: who is a real monk?

He brings in a key scriptural support:

Āchārāṅg Sūtra: “A true monk is one who abides in his Soul.”

Implications:

  • Only someone who has experienced the Soul can abide in the Soul.
  • So:
    • Someone may be living with strict discipline,
    • But if he hasn’t realised the Self,
    • He does not meet the scriptural definition of a true monk.

Thus:

“Atmajnān tyā munipanu” = Where there is Self-realisation, there is (true) monkhood.

Meaning:

  • Monkhood ought to be accompanied by Self-realisation.
  • Without that:
    • We can respectfully say: “He practices renunciation, discipline.”
    • But not: “He is a genuine monk in the full scriptural sense.”

10. Two natural questions – answered from this verse

He acknowledges the confusion this verse can create and explicitly poses two questions:

Q1: So are all monks Self-realised?

  • No.
  • Self-realisation is not:
    • Automatically granted by draping certain clothes,
    • Or just receiving the initiation symbol.
  • Renunciation is very good, but: Becoming a monk does not automatically mean Self-realisation.

So:

All monks need not be Self-realised. So don’t blindly treat every monk as Sadguru.

Q2: Are all Self-realised souls monks?

  • Also no.
  • Someone may:
    • Attain Self-realisation (4th guṇasthān),
    • Yet be outwardly a householder,
    • Not formally initiated as a monk.

So he asks:

“Can I take such a Self-realised householder as my Sadguru or not?”

This is where he brings in the crucial concept of saintliness (sādhutā).

11. Self-realisation is always accompanied by sādhutā (saintliness)

He gives the key insight:

“Self-realisation is always accompanied by saintliness.”

Meaning:

  • One who has experienced the Self is:
    • A real Saint (whether in robes or not).
  • A real Saint is:
    • A true Guru.

Differences may only be in:

  • Abidance:
    • Some wear the “spectacles of awareness” for:
      • A few minutes a day,
      • Some for hours,
      • Some almost continuously.
  • But:
    • Once Enlightenment happens, the eyes have opened.
    • He knows the full path up to liberation.

So:

Where there is Self-realisation, there is sādhutā (saintliness). Where there is true saintliness, there is Sadguru-hood.

Thus the verse is not saying:

  • “Only a person in a certain dress can be Guru.”

It is saying:

  • Self-realisation is the core,
  • And true monkhood and Sadguru-hood are defined by that, not by clothing.

12. “Kulguru kalpanā” – tradition, family Guru & moving on

Second half of the verse:

“Bākī kulguru kalpanā, ātmārthī nahi joy.”

He explains:

  • To follow someone as Guru purely because:
    • “He is the family Guru,”
    • “Our ancestors always followed this person/lineage,”
    • While knowing he has no Self-realisation,
  • Is called kalpanā:
    • Projection,
    • Imagination,
    • Not Truth.

Daily-life examples he gives:

  • Your great-grandfather travelled in a horse cart (Victoria).
    • Do you feel guilty for travelling by car now? No.
  • They all wore dhotis.
    • You wear pants, shirts, suits.
  • Food:
    • Earlier: only bhakri.
    • Now: you even turn bhakri into pizza for kids.
  • So practically in life:
    • You have moved ahead from tradition.

He says:

If changing vehicles and clothes is acceptable, why freeze your spiritual understanding at your great-grandfather’s level?

He is not asking you to rebel out of anger, but:

“If now, through Param Krupalu Dev, you understand the real path and the true nature of Sadguru, then don’t let blind family loyalty stop you from walking that path.”

13. How an ātmārthī relates to different people

The verse’s spirit shows in his behaviour:

Toward monks & nuns:

  • He offers:
    • Respect,
    • Sādharmik bhakti,
    • Swamivātsalya (feeding seekers),
    • Seva.
  • He values:
    • Their effort,
    • Their discipline,
    • Their study.

Toward learned scholars:

  • He honours:
    • Their scriptural knowledge.

But “Sadguru” is reserved:

  • Only for one he is convinced is:
    • Self-realised,
    • Living with true sādhutā.

So he:

1. Examines with discrimination (vivek):

  • “Is this person Self-realised?”

2. When convinced:

  • Takes refuge (śaraṇ),
  • Obeys āgnā (commands),
  • Allows himself to be led to liberation.

This shows:

  • Inner maturity,
  • Discriminating vision (vivek),
  • Courage to choose truth over mere tradition.

14. The heart of verse 34 – in one shot

Verse 34 as the speaker is presenting it:

A true seeker (ātmārthī) starts his journey with a clear, uncompromising understanding of a Sadguru:

  • Self-realisation is essential.
  • True monkhood = where there is Self-realisation.
  • A true Guru is a Self-realised Saint, whether in robes or in household life.
  • Merely following a family/sect Guru without Self-realisation is kalpanā – a nice imagination but not the path to liberation.

He honours everyone, but surrenders only to one who truly abides in the Self. This clarity saves him from bigotry, blind following, and wasted effort, and plants his feet firmly on the direct path to Self-realisation and liberation.
